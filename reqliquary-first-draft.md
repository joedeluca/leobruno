A cold drizzle has been falling since I began my descent from Eremo delle Carceri. The scaglia rossa gleams pink and white under my feet and I'm thinking about that Žižek thing—pubic hair, ideology, the obscene underside—and I want to bury myself in her-just _that_. The thought arrives unbidden. The _Real_ breaking through. Mouth, legs, breasts, eyes blacked with mascara streaked with sweat.

And the limestone beneath me accumulated as foraminifera shells on the Tethys Sea floor for one hundred and eighty million years. Layer upon layer. Death becoming stone. Then thrust upward thirty-five million years ago when Africa crumpled into Europe.

And I want to see secret things. I want to peel back each layer one at a time and see them with Actaeon's eyes feasting on the nymphs of Artemis and Artemis herself and then get _stagged_.

Legs shaking from the run up. The controlled fall—resisting gravity while surrendering to it. Be present. Rock. Breath. Rock. But my head feels cracked. Full of static. Broke.

And Pearl in Chapel Hill with Rachel, my _Ex_-wife. Like calling limestone 'former seafloor.' Technically accurate but missing the violence of the transformation. She's fifteen now. Is she thinking about me? Probably not. She's thinking about her own life.

The Umbrian polyculture patchwork of green and gold flashes past through the breaks and elicits nothing but remembering.

Sheep in the valley become the Nelson Atkins lawn and Moore's bronze sheep engorged, mounting. _Sheep Piece_. Mimi, Walt, before our hail Mary-Pearl-was born, running through sprinklers on that vast lawn. The smell of patchouli oil and cut grass. Our art ghetto dream fractured into glass towers and law firm parties.

And why do I keep seeing the _Anna Karenina_ she left by the bed for a year, bookmark stuck in the middle, gathering dust; some pathetic relic marking the gut punch realization that she moved differently in the dark. I knew before I knew and even when I did know I did nothing except rot in silence for twenty years.

The impact tears through my running shorts and climbs—wrist, elbow, shoulder. Slipping, scraping. Down before I realize. Twisted against the gorgon roots. Don't look at the damage, keep moving. The pain is a release.

Tired of thinking. Tired of talking. The dogs in Assisi know. We exchange scents, mark, move.

Am I present on the wrong plane? The brain censors itself in dreams. Nympholepsy. Sexmosaic. Nymphomanic vision. Twisted shapes, disintegrating wholeness. Machines producing flows everywhere.

At Rocca Minore, the trailhead, my legs shaking, sky darkening, I look for the bat colony in the tower gaps, but they're staying in, out of the rain. Through Porte Cappuccini and down into Assisi—I'm filled with a visceral infusion of absurd heroism, descending through the empty city. The tourists gone. The shops closed. Just me and the wet mysteries.

# Powderfinger

Elliott is waiting outside St. Leonard's when we emerge from the service, leaning against the limestone wall with his arms crossed, reading glasses perched on the end of his nose as he reads a slim paperback—_The Wolf and Other Stories_ by Giacomo Pieri. Grey cashmole sweater, colorful scarf today (burnt orange and yellow, like leaves). If you want to imagine a Hollywood actor for Elliott, make it Kevin Kline. He has both the physical presence and charm to pull everyone into his orbit.

He glances up when he hears us, closes the book carefully—using his finger to mark the page—and smiles. "Olivia," he says warmly. "You look lovely."

My mother, who'd been tense through the entire service, relaxes. "Oh. Thank you." She does look lovely. Classic Italian-American beauty—the kind that could stop traffic in her day.

"How was it?" He falls into step beside us as we start down the steep brick path. Each brick inscribed with a patron's name—donations to the Basilica, buying proximity to Francis's bones. Death stacked on death stacked on payment for redemption.

"It was good," she says, then adds, because Elliott has a way of making her try: "Father Tim preached on forgiveness."

Elliott got here two years ago—six months before I did. Retired from advertising. Rachel and I met him in an Italian class—he was fluent already, just there to meet people. When we showed up with Pearl, scrambling for an apartment, he introduced us to Yuki and Francesco.

We'd meet for coffee, gossip, and talk about daily life. He'd been sober fifteen years by then, had a sponsor in New York he Skyped with twice a week, did the morning pages, the whole program. When Rachel and I were still together and Pearl was here, she would often hang out with us and he would keep her laughing.

But really, Eli is just unfailingly kind. He allows me space when I weep at moons. When I see an old woman in a sexy skirt and catalogue her every detail like god's scribe documenting beauty for some galactic ledger. When I talk to animals—ducks, mostly. Fucking ducks. There's a flock outside the walls. You should hear the racket when they see me coming.

This is why I run to the point of exhaustion every day. I don't want to be this person—this manic, babbling thing—and I don't want to take the drugs. I want to be a hidden door with no handle. I want to be elevator music. Running discharges some of the voltage. Deleuze said the schizophrenic out for a walk is a better model than a neurotic lying on the analyst's couch—said like a man who never felt the tsunamic terror of feeling everything all at once. I'm not running toward the flows, I'm running away from them. Trying to burn off the excess before I become the thing people point at, before I start weeping at moons and talking to ducks in public where everyone can see.

"That's a nice sweater," my mother says, her voice plastic. "Is it new?"

"Yuki showed me this outlet place," Elliott says, reaching for her purse without being asked. She lets him take it. "Outside Perugia. Everything's half price if you don't mind _last season_."

"Oh." She nods, not following the conversation, or that he was being silly when he says last season like he's reading a children's book and last year's model is a monster under the bed. Her smile fixed. "That's nice."

This is how my mom holds it together. She keeps to her own script. Usually.

"Oh, before I forget," Elliott says, adjusting his satchel. "This one's for you, Leo." He hands me the book he'd been reading. "Found it in that bookshop in Perugia. Stories about medieval saints—thought you'd like it."

"It's been translated," Elliott says. "So you're in luck. There's one about Francis and a wolf in Gubbio. Stark. Unsentimental. Not the Franciscan Disneyland version."

My mother will never have a brick. Neither will I. We're just walking on other people's purchased immortality, descending through their prayers toward her apartment where thousands of paintings nobody's seen are stacked against walls.

Men who used fists like punctuation marks. Stepfather with a wooden leg and a heroin habit—I slept on the couch, heard everything, used headphones and Neil Young to block it out. My mother paints it. Can't speak it, but she paints it. Every canvas an exorcism. Every ascending fairy a middle finger to the men who thought they owned her body. Every bubble on every tit a reclamation of pleasure that was stolen from her at twelve.

"There's a chapel," she says after a few minutes, pointing. Her voice is back to careful. "I never noticed it before."

It's tucked into the wall, easy to miss. Small wooden door, partially hidden by an olive tree, half-swallowed by fog. More limestone. Everything here is limestone.

"Want to look inside?" I ask.

She doesn't answer. Just walks toward it.

The door is unlocked. We follow her in.

It's tiny—maybe fifteen feet across. Stone walls, one small window, afternoon light coming through dusty air. And against the far wall: a life-size crucifix. Christ suffering, realistic wounds, crown of thorns, the whole thing. Caravaggio painted saints in ecstasy that looked like orgasm. This Christ looks like he's drowning.

My mother stops in the doorway.

Then she walks forward, and I see it happening before it happens. Her knees give. She's on the floor, then on her face, arms spread out, prostrate before a god who died two thousand years ago.

She's weeping. Not crying—weeping. Deep, racking sobs. Her whole body shaking.

Elliott and I look at each other.

She's praying. I can hear fragments. English and Latin mixed together, half-remembered prayers from childhood, from before everything broke, from when she still believed something could save her.

"Jesus Christ our Lord..."

"...mea culpa..."

"...I can't..."

"...please..."

Her voice is breaking. She can't catch her breath.

"Mom." I kneel beside her. "Come on."

She won't move. Dead weight.

"Let's help you up," Elliott says gently. He's on her other side.

Between us we manage to lift her. She's not cooperating but she's not resisting either. Just limp, still crying, mascara running down her face.

We get my mother standing. She wipes her eyes with her hands, smearing the makeup. Looks at the crucifix, then at us, then away.

She doesn't say anything. Just lets us guide her outside.

We walk her out. She won't look at us. Her face is blotchy, mascara everywhere, scarf askew. She pulls away, walks a few steps ahead, wiping her face with a tissue. Trying to fix her makeup without a mirror. The fog presses against us, turning everything gray.

After a few minutes she stops, turns back. "It's beautiful though. You have to admit. All that suffering. They knew how to make suffering beautiful."

"It is beautiful," Elliott agrees.

"But it's not real. None of it's real." She says it flatly. "Just theater."

"Beautiful theater," Elliott says.

She looks at him. "Yeah. Beautiful theater."

# CHAPTER 8: The Wolf

# The Wolf

\begin{center}
From \textit{Racconti Umbri} by Giacomo Pieri (1891) \\
Translated by Margaret Whitmore (1964)
\end{center}

The town wore winter like a funeral shroud. Its roofs and chimneys thrust through freshly fallen snow like rotting fingers of the buried alive. Stone walls the color of old bone enclosed it. Beyond the eastern gate, the mountain rose steep and forested—bare trees like cracks in glass, shadows pooling in the ravines where streams ran frozen. No movement. No tracks leading out. The town had sealed itself.

The church bells rang for Epiphany. Inside the stone houses, people broke bread and poured wine with unsteady hands. Outside, the snow held its silence.

Something was killing. Had killed six times since the spring feast of gentle Ubaldo. At first it was livestock, and then towns people; bodies found in the snow or not found at all. Just blood. Just drag marks leading into the trees where no one followed. All agreed, satan was at hand. There had not been breeze to spin a leaf for weeks. They were stranded inside a demonic doldrum.

The fighting men stayed inside. They drank wine and played dice and talked strategy, of waiting for spring, of protocol. One does not hunt demons, they said. One waits for the Church. One sends for proper authorities. One does not venture into cursed wood in winter on the word of peasants who saw shadows.

From the belltower you could see the town's smallness. How the forest surrounded it on three sides. How the mountain rose behind, white and black and indifferent. How the single road wound up from the valley to the north gate, and how nothing moved on that road. Not for days. Not until the morning of Epiphany.

The acolyte kept watch from the belfry.

A speck at first. Black against white. Coming down from the mountain forests. Not rushing. Not hesitating. Just walking. He took its full measure and when he was sure, cried out.

The saint, returning from the wilderness. The saint, who had gone looking for it. The saint, covered in mud and possibly blood.

---

The boy woke in darkness and the cold had teeth. A freeze that came once in a generation, the old woman said, though she'd said it before. The stones of the hut held the cold like they'd been born to it. He could hear her breathing in the dark, shallow and catching.

No firewood for three days. The thing had taken the woman at the forest edge and after that no one went beyond the walls. The boy had gone once at dawn, crept to where the deadfall lay near the stream, but he'd heard something moving in the ravine. Not animal sounds. Something unnatural. A breath. A coil of word sounds that evaporated instantly. He ran. Left the wood there. Returned with nothing.

The old woman didn't say anything about it. She just wrapped herself tighter in the thin blanket and watched him.

They had a handful of charcoal left from the forge. The blacksmith's boy had sold it to them for the old woman's copper pot. The boy built a small fire in the brazier and sealed the door with rags. The thing was out there, he heard it sniffing at the crevices.

The priest had told a story in church. About a holy man who found a raven eating the communion host. The bird was possessed, the priest said. Demon-worn. The holy man caught it and plucked out every feather, one by one, until the shrieking bird was naked and bloody. Then he left it in the road to flail about until the cats found it and devoured it. Such was the power of god over demons and the weak nature of lower beasts, which have no souls to guard them and so let devils in. Women, he said, had similarly weak natures.

The boy thought about that raven sometimes. And about the old woman's nature, which seemed to him the most inviolable thing in Gubbio. Did women have souls? He couldn't remember.

In the smoke-thickened air he saw the saint walking down from the mountain.

Barefoot through snow. Feet leaving prints that filled with blood. Something followed him.

Something massive. Wrapped in fur and rags. Moving on two legs but not like a man moves. Moving with two rhythms—a broken internal rhythm like a spinning wheel coming off its spool, the other inevitable, patient.

"Is he back?" the old woman asked. Her voice came from far away.

"Who?"

"The brother. The one who went looking."

Yes. The saint was closer now. Face young and ruined. What followed marked him like a shadow. Huge and hunched. Its breath like laughter. Or weeping.

The fire burned low. The room was warm now. Warmer than it had been in days.

The boy felt sleepy.

The old woman's breathing had gone quiet. Or maybe he just couldn't hear it anymore. Over the sound of the saint walking. The crunch of snow under his bare feet.

The saint stopped walking. Turned to face what followed. Knelt down in the snow in the valley between the pines.

It stood over him.

Wearing pelts. Or it was pelts. Where the fur ended and the flesh began—

It raised something. Hand. Claw. Blade.

The saint didn't pull away. Didn't move. Just knelt there as the thing descended like nightfall.

The boy wanted to call out but his throat—

The room was so warm now. Warm as spring. Warm as being inside something.

Silence fell again. Worse than the noise. The kind of silence that has weight.

The saint walked through.

The others hung back at the edge.

Then the saint stopped.

Everything stopped.

Dropped to his knees in the snow.

Empty.

Palms up.

Too far away.

Too slow.

Offering nothing.

Slowly.

Saw it change.

Something worse.

Recognition.

Walked past the thing and headed back up the slope to the eastern gate.

Where is it?

Did you kill it?

It's still there. In the ravine.

It won't come into the town again.

How do you know?

The saint looked at the man who asked. The butcher. The one who'd lost his son.

Because I know.

That's not an answer.

The saint nodded. No. It's not.

He turned and went into the church.

The door closed behind him.

The people stood in the street and looked at each other and no one knew what to do.

Suddenly the boy saw the raven. Naked in the snow. Wings broken. Crawling through fog.

The saint emerged from the white and picked up the raven and then disappeared with it.

The boy's throat was closing. His chest tight. The room so warm. The fire so low. The old woman's breathing so quiet.

The rags had fallen away.

The brazier was cold. Its last coals gone to ash.

He was lying on the floor and he didn't remember lying down.

The old woman sat in her chair by the wall. Still wrapped in her blanket. She was looking at him but not seeing him.

She'd gone still the way things go still when they're done with moving.

The boy stood up.

His head swam.

He went to the door and pulled it open and the cold air came in like a fist hitting him in the chest and he fell to his knees in the doorway and vomited into the snow.

Red against white.

The only color in the world.

# CHAPTER 9: Sokushinbutsu

# Sokushinbutsu

A vase on a shelf in her grandmother's apartment in Osaka. Celadon glaze. The crack running through it repaired with gold lacquer—_kintsugi_. Her grandmother said the crack made it more valuable. The breaking made it whole.

Yuki was seven when her grandmother showed her how to be still. Sit. Don't move. Let the room move around you. The traffic outside, the neighbors, the television from upstairs—let it all move while you stay.

She learned. She could sit for an hour without fidgeting. Her mother called it unnatural. Her grandmother called it a gift.

At eighteen in California she was still sitting. Still learning to hold space. The restaurant where she worked smelled like ginger and fryer oil. The regular customers knew her name. The irregulars pointed at the menu like children.

When Francesco walked in that first time, she knew immediately: tourist, lost, beautiful in that particular way of men who've stopped trying. He pointed at something on the menu—the wrong thing, something no one ordered—and she laughed. He laughed too without knowing why.

---

We're halfway up Monte Subasio when I realize Yuki has been walking in silence for twenty minutes. She's wearing vintage hiking boots, black stretch shorts, and a loose floral dress that barely covers her ass. And of course those giant red-rimmed glasses that in the wilderness seem even more exotic—a figure out of time and place, a western tanager from California on the trail Francis walked eight hundred years ago.

She stops suddenly, crouches beside a boulder, touches the green-gray crust spreading across its surface.

"That takes two hundred years to grow," I say.

She looks up at me. Doesn't blink. Just looks. In that look I understand: she already knew. About the lichen. About me.

She stands and keeps walking.

The trees thin out. The landscape opens into something lunar—white and pink stone, scrub grass, sky so blue it hurts. And horses. Wild horses that shouldn't exist this high, this far from water, but they're here anyway, grazing on nothing, watching us with ancient indifference.

We round a bend and nearly collide with them—three Italian tourists coming down, dressed like they're heading to Sunday mass. The woman in ballet flats, picking her way across the rocks. The men in slacks and leather loafers. One of them is holding a guidebook open to a page that says _Sentiero di San Francesco: Via Facile all'Eremo delle Carceri_—Francis's path, easy route to the hermitage. He's looking at it like it's personally betrayed him.

"Scusa," the woman says, breathless, pointing back up the trail. "Quanto... how far?"

I start to answer but my Italian tangles in my mouth. She looks at me, waiting, and I feel Yuki's presence beside me shift.

"Ancora quarantacinque minuti," Yuki says, her Italian sudden and flawless. "Forse cinquanta con queste scarpe." She glances at the woman's shoes, not unkindly. "La guida dice 'facile' ma è facile per alpinisti, non per turisti. Molti tornano indietro."

The man with the guidebook laughs, bitter but grateful. "Dovevamo saperlo."

"C'è un bar in Assisi," Yuki continues, gesturing down the mountain. "Chiedete del Café Duomo. Dite che Yuki vi ha mandato. Vi faranno un buon prezzo."

They thank her, nod to me with that particular Italian politeness that says they've already forgotten I exist, and continue their careful descent.

Yuki watches them go, then looks at me.

"Your Italian is getting better," she says. Not true. "But sometimes it's easier if I just do it."

She starts walking again.

I watch the tourists pick their way down. They came for transcendence not suffering.

Ten minutes of silence. Then Yuki stops again.

"Wait. Listen."

I stop. The wind has died. Complete absence of sound.

"Nothing," she says. "That's the sound. In California there was always a highway somewhere. Here you can hear nothing."

We walk on. She doesn't speak again for another ten minutes.

When she finally stops at a flat outcropping overlooking the valley, she doesn't turn around. She speaks to the air, to the horses, to something I can't see.

"I was still a child. A shape waiting to be filled. Not empty—I wasn't empty. I was ready. Like a vase sitting in a room, waiting for someone to notice it could hold something. The first thing that arrives, you think that's the thing. You don't know you can choose what gets poured in. You just know you're ready to hold it."

I don't say anything. I've learned by now that Yuki's silences and Yuki's speaking come from the same place, and when you find her in that place you know that she cannot be interrupted in either her silence or her words. She cannot be directed or redirected.

"Francesco came to the restaurant where I worked. He ordered wrong. Pointed at the menu like a child. I laughed. He laughed too. He didn't understand why we were laughing but he laughed."

She sits on the rock, legs folded beneath her. I stay standing.

"He was beautiful then. Fifty but beautiful. The way some Italian men are beautiful when they stop trying." She glances up at me, her eyes magnified behind those thick red-rimmed glasses. "I thought he was rich. He dressed rich. He moved rich. Later I understood—he moved like an Assisiano. They all move like that. Like the stones belong to them."

She glances down at her feet, notices her own sandals.

"These shoes belonged to Francesco's first wife. She left them. Thirty years ago. They fit me perfectly." Her voice goes somewhere else. "I think about her feet sometimes. Walking where I walk."

A horse moves closer, close enough I can see the muscles shift beneath its coat. Yuki reaches into her bag, pulls out an apple, holds it flat on her palm. The horse considers, then takes it with lips that barely touch her skin.

"And you married him?" The question comes out before I can stop it.

"I married what I saw. What I saw was not what was there." She stands, wipes her hands on her dress. "He didn't speak good English. I thought his silence was depth. I learned Italian to find out what he was thinking."

"What was he thinking?"

"Nothing complicated. Mass on Sunday. Pasta with the right sauce. A wife who is quiet. Children who obey. The old things. The Catholic things. The things his mother believed and her mother before." She starts walking again, along the ridge. "I was twenty-three when I understood this. Five years to learn a language. Five years to discover I had married a simple man."

"Were you angry?"

She stops. Turns. Looks at me directly for the first time since we started climbing.

"Angry. Yes. For one week I was angry. Then I was something else." She pinches her fingers together, showing me something shrinking into nothing. "You know what I did? I made myself smaller. Every day a little smaller. Like this. My painting—smaller. My music—smaller. The things I wanted—smaller and smaller until they fit inside the life he had made for us."

The wind picks up. Her dress flattens against her body, and she doesn't move to hold it down.

"That sounds—"

"It sounds terrible. Yes. But also it was peaceful."

She walks again. I follow. A hawk circles overhead, riding thermals we can't feel from down here.

Yuki stops, watches it.

"My father said when you see a hawk you should tell it a secret. Out loud. Because hawks carry secrets to the dead."

She's quiet for a long moment, still watching.

"I've never done it. I don't know who I would send a secret to."

More horses appear on the ridge above us, their manes dark against the sky.

"His daughters came in summer. Lucia and Marta. American girls. Their mother had left him too—did I say that? She went back to Philadelphia. So the girls came to me." Her voice softens. "I taught them violin. I taught them Italian. I taught them to fold napkins and set a table and be quiet when their father was tired. They were my only children. I loved them like my own."

"Where are they now?"

"Grown. Lucia in Milano. Marta in London. They call on my birthday. They send photographs of their children. Francesco doesn't remember their names anymore. I remind him."

We've reached a plateau—flat stone stretching out like a stage, the valley spread below us in that Umbrian patchwork of green and gold that used to move me and now just reminds me of everything I've lost.

She stops, breathes deeply through her nose.

"Rosemary. Wild. My grandmother in Osaka grew rosemary in a pot on her balcony. She had never been to Italy. She didn't know it grew wild here like a weed. She thought it was precious. She watered it every day. When she died my mother threw it away."

Something moves in the brush below. A boar maybe, or just wind through the scrub.

Yuki crouches, points at something on the ground.

"Horse. Fresh. This morning maybe." She glances up at me. "My father could tell what an animal ate by looking at its shit. He grew up on a farm. "His memory."

"Yes. Going away. Slowly. Like water down a drain. You don't see it leave but one day the tub is empty." She pulls her knees up, wraps her arms around them. "He started sleeping on his Bible. "I watch him become old. Every day a little more. The body forgetting how to be young."

A horse appears around the bend in front of us—white with gray dappling, massive and silent. It stops. Looks at us. We look back. Some agreement is reached. It moves on.

"Look," Yuki whispers, though it's already gone.

"You know what I thought about those years?" Yuki says finally. "The years of making myself smaller?"

# CHAPTER 12: CHAPTER 12: Santo Stefano di Sessanio

# CHAPTER 12: Santo Stefano di Sessanio

The village is medieval. Stone houses built into the mountainside. Population maybe two hundred. No hotels. No souvenir shops. Just shepherds and old women in black and one trattoria serving wild boar ragù by a roaring fireplace.

We arrive late afternoon. The light already dying. Our place is a cave, basically—stone walls, low ceilings, a wood stove the young landlady lights for us before she leaves. She shows us how to feed it, where the good bread is in the morning, when she'll come back to make breakfast. Her hands are rough. Mountain hands.

That first night we walk the empty streets. Roxy wrapped in her sweater, me in my jacket. The village is silent. No cars. No music. Just our footsteps on cobblestones and, through lit windows, locals watching TV in their living rooms. We're the only ones outside.

We eat at the trattoria. Wood fire. Wild boar. Montepulciano d'Abruzzo that tastes like the mountains. The owner doesn't speak English. Roxy orders in Italian but I can hear her Sardinian accent pushing through. He smiles. Asks where she's from. They talk about islands and mountains and how people from both places understand stone.

Across the table, by candlelight and fireplace, she's explaining something about Supramonte—the grand canyon of Italy. How she and her brother used to chase sheep into the chasm when they were children. How they'd climb trees and dangle their legs over nothing. How Sardegna is not really Italy.

---

### The Ride

Morning. The landlady makes us breakfast. Bread. Pecorino. Honey. Espresso in tiny cups. She brings it to our stone cave and watches Roxy prepare her bike. Adjusts something on the pannier. Nods.

The ride is twelve hours. Brutal terrain. Trails that climb through beech forests where the light comes through in shafts like in paintings. Technical descents on loose shale where one wrong move sends you over. Views of Gran Sasso that look like Tolkien drew them—jagged, impossible, hostile, beautiful.

We outflank vicious dogs guarding sheep. Split through herds of cattle with bulls that watch us pass, deciding whether to charge. We navigate scree fields and river crossings and sections so steep I have to carry the bike on my shoulder while Roxy powers ahead, reading terrain like sheet music.

She's faster on climbs. More technical on descents. My attention drifts, I crash on flat ground. I crash on descents. I fall over while eating a peanut butter and jelly sandwich along side the trail.

I'm bruised and bloodied and I think this reminds of after a high school football game. She's tired too—I can see it in how she moves—but she's smiling.

We stop once. High ridge. Thousand-meter drop. She drinks water. Eats nothing. Looks out at the mountains and says nothing.

---

### Rocca Calascio

Hour eight or nine—I've lost count—we climb to Rocca Calascio. The fortress. Roxy told me about it last night. Built in the tenth century. Maybe earlier. No one knows for sure.

We push the bikes up the final approach. Too steep to ride. Path made of loose stone. My calves are screaming. Roxy's ahead, always ahead, but even she's breathing hard now.

And then we're there. At the top. And the world unfolds.

The fortress ruins rise up like broken teeth against the sky. Four cylindrical towers at the corners, partially collapsed, stones the color of the mountain itself. Beyond: the Gran Sasso massif, snow-capped even in late spring, impossibly close, like you could reach out and touch it. The Corno Grande. The Corno Piccolo.

Below us, valleys drop away in every direction. Green. Wild. Empty.

I look at Roxy. She's standing at the edge, with her helmet off, wind pulling at her curly black hair. Small. Silhouetted against mountains and sky. She turns. Sees me. Smiles.

"_Eccoci_," she says. Here we are.

The wind. The ruins. The mountains. This woman who child-locked me in her car yesterday, standing here in this impossible place she planned for us.

Santo Stefano di Sessanio is down there somewhere.

Roxy walks back to me. Takes my hand. Says nothing. We stand there. Wind. Ruins. Mountains. Sky. I want her hand in mine.

---

### After

We get back at dusk. The owner has lit a fire and left wood by the stove. Roxy showers first. Long. I can hear the water running. When she comes out she's wearing nothing but her bathrobe—thick white terrycloth, too big for her.

She sits by the stove. Doesn't speak. Looks at her phone. Scrolling through nothing.

I see her skin in the firelight. Already dark—that Sardinian darkness that makes people ask where she's from—but darker now where her cycling gear left her exposed. Her forearms. Her calves. Her face. And where her clothes were, where the sun couldn't reach, she's pink and white by comparison.

I kneel between her legs. Open her robe.

She closes her eyes. Puts the phone down. Doesn't speak.

The stone walls are two feet thick. No sound from outside. The wood stove crackling. Her breathing. Mine.

After, we lie on the narrow bed. Her back against my chest. The robe still open. Fire dying. She's already half asleep.

"Grazie," she says. Quiet.

"Per cosa?"

"For coming. For trying."

I don't say anything.

We're trying. Both of us. Building a bridge across a chasm that might not have a bottom.

The weekend is incredible. And it's doomed. And I can see both things at once.

Sunday morning. Packing. The landlady brings more coffee, bread, honey. She and Roxy talk about the trails, the weather, whether the bears are coming down from higher elevations this year.

I'm sore everywhere. Bruised.

We load the car in silence. Not the tense silence of Friday morning. Different.

The drive back: no child locks. No French audiobooks. Just Roxy driving fast, competent, hugging curves, and me in the passenger seat watching Abruzzo turn back into Le Marche turn back into Umbria. Medieval villages passing. The Apennines receding behind us.

"That was good," she says finally. We're an hour into the drive. First words spoken.

"It was."

"But you're thinking something."

"I'm always thinking something."

"Tell me."

"I've been married," I say. "Twenty-eight years. Three kids. I've lived with someone who knows all my patterns. My moods. When I need space. When I need pulling back. I've lived in a house where someone knows which coffee cup I use. Which side of the bed. How I take my eggs."

"So?"

"So you haven't. You've lived alone your whole life. Everything organized. Everything controlled. You don't know what it's like to—" I stop.

"To what?"

"To compromise. Every day. To give up the perfect angle on things for the messy shared thing."

She's quiet. Driving. The autostrada smooth under us. Umbria spreading out ahead.

"So you think I can't," she finally says. Not a question.

"I think you haven't. And I think you're fifty."

"And you?" She glances at me. "You think your patterns are better? The notebooks full of women you catalog like butterflies? The obsessing? The wanting what you can't have?"

I search for the word. "We're both too late. Too formed. Too ourselves."

She drives. Doesn't argue. Doesn't defend.

After a while: "Maybe that's true. Maybe we're too old to change enough." She shifts gears, passes a truck. "But the weekend was good."

"It is."

I'll carry this for weeks. Months. Write about it in notebooks she'll never see. Make it mean something it doesn't mean.

She'll just remember it as a good weekend. And move on.

# CHAPTER 14: The Wedding

# The Wedding

Ontogeny recapitulates phylogeny. The individual development mirrors the evolutionary history of the species. It's not quite true—Haeckel fudged his drawings, made the embryos look more similar than they are—but the principle holds in rough form. You can see the fish in the fetus, the gill slits that become jaw parts, the tail that mostly reabsorbs. Your daughter carries you forward not just genetically but structurally, the shape of your thinking folded into her cells.

Mimi is a librarian. She inherited my atomic number—the same obsessive mind but grounded, functional. The way of feeling intensely, every connection a live wire, the inability to leave an idea alone, the compulsion to follow every thread until it unravels or weaves into something true. She got the full neural package but with the ability to actually use it. When she was seven she asked me why Christians kept pieces of dead people in churches and I explained reliquaries and she said, "So it's like keeping great grandma's jewelry but grosser and more official?" and I knew she'd gotten it, gotten the entire question of presence and absence, of what remains when the person is gone, of how we try to make the holy portable.

She's twenty-seven now. Getting married today. To a man who studies organisms so intensely he can identify fungi by smell, who once spent twenty minutes explaining the symbiotic relationship between fig wasps and fig trees at Thanksgiving dinner while everyone else ate pie and then sank into comatose wasp dreams; who called me from Chapel Hill six months ago and asked—actually asked, voice shaking slightly—if he could marry my daughter. That phone call. That impossible, archaic, beautiful gesture that nobody does anymore.

His name is Declan. Thick-set, bearded, from Michigan originally, the kind of Midwestern friendly that seems fake until you realize it's load-bearing. Mimi says he's the first man she's dated who isn't performing anything. A dictionary of organisms.

Rachel paid for everything. The whole wedding. Didn't make it weird. Just said she wanted to and could afford it and would Mimi please let her because it would make her happy. She makes the kind of money I never made as an adjunct professor bouncing between gigs, never made as a technical writer for software companies, never made doing any of the things I did to keep us afloat while I told myself I was still an artist. She doesn't hold it against me anymore. Or if she does, she's kind enough not to show it.

Forty people flew in from America for this. Mostly Mimi and Declan's friends from Brooklyn—the crew from their grad school years, library science and biology students who became a kind of chosen family. As a group, they radiate confident American energy. Young Italians seem quieter by contrast. Like the medieval chain of being never quite dissolved here and these muscular, a quiet contrast to these muscular and loud and bursting with life New Yorkers.

They've been here a week, touring Umbria, asking about organic farms and whether the pasta is made with eggs. My apartment is four blocks from where the ceremony was supposed to happen, but they're staying at an agriturismo outside town, something with a pool and vineyard views that Rachel booked. I took them to the top of Monte Subasio and we walked among the horses. The chased them trying to get on one. The horses were confused, having never seen humans with cowboy blood.

These are my people. I feel it. The weird American world of extreme diversity, earnest about composting and correct language and showing up for each other. But I know I'm not going back. Ever. I don't understand why yet, but I feel it inside as a certainty. I'm going to go deeper into this. Into this strange and wonderful place where I understand nothing. Italians fascinate me the way bats do—beautiful black mysteries. Here I am the foreigner, the outsider, the one who will never quite belong. Something about that appeals to me.

The original plan: outdoor wedding at a scenic overlook about fifteen kilometers from Assisi. Mimi's only requirement was "nothing iconic." Her word. She didn't want the Basilica, didn't want anything tourists photograph, didn't want spectacle. Just nature, sky, her people. "Papa, I don't want pretension," she said when we talked logistics. "I want trees and grass and Declan reading some crazy poem from the stone age. That's it."

I understood. For her, coming to Italy was the vacation beforehand, the time with her friends and family. The actual wedding was to be simple, direct, and free of what she called "vomitous bride tropes." She has my allergy to performance.

But three days ago the forecast turned. Today it's been raining since dawn. Not drizzle—sheets of water, wind tearing at awnings, the kind of storm that makes medieval cities look medieval again, all the centuries showing at once. At 6 a.m. I got the call. Rachel, efficient: "We need an indoor venue. Can you find something? We can pay whatever." And I said, "I know a place. Give me a minute."

I walked to Santa Margherita in the rain wearing my pajamas and slippers. Early enough that no one was there, just the old woman who cleans, who knows me because she passes my window every day. I explained in broken Italian. Wedding. Today. American daughter. Weather bad. She called the priest. He said yes. Of course yes. The church is small, mostly empty, glad for use. One hundred euro donation. I gave two hundred.

Santa Margherita. The church at the end of my vicolo. The plain one tourists miss because it's not Francis, not Chiara, not painted by Giotto. Just a small church for a medieval woman who never had any status, was a mistress to a murdered nobleman who lost with him all of her worldly possessions, who found his body in the woods led there by his dog, who walked back to her village and confessed her sexual sins publicly during Mass, who reinvented herself through poverty and service, who founded a hospital and an order of nuns, who became—centuries after she died—a saint.

The church for people who can't start from moral clarity. The saint for the compromised. Margaret of Cortona, who made beauty from wreckage.

I thought: Mimi didn't want iconic. She wanted honest. This has at least the germ of it.

By 10 a.m. the Americans were arriving, shaking out umbrellas, laughing at the weather, saying things like "Pioggia ridicoloso" and "Molto crap-o this-o" Rachel showed up in something cream-colored and expensive, managing her own umbrella, and somehow looking more Italian than an Italian. With her: a man. Middle-aged, never had children. Open face, bumbling demeanor that seemed to indicate personal assistant, not lover.

Rachel sees me. Walks over. The man follows.

"Leo, this is Edison. Edison, Leo."

We shake hands. His grip is firm but not performative.

"It's an honor," he says. "Rachel talks about you all the time. And Mimi. This whole family, really. I feel like I already know you."

I notice immediately that he talks a lot.

"Thanks for coming."

"Wouldn't miss it." He looks at the church. "This is beautiful. Really. The simplicity of it. Rachel said you found it this morning?"

"It's my vicolo. Stone's throw."

"That's perfect. That's exactly right." He's nodding too much, the way people do when they're nervous but trying not to show it.

Rachel touches his arm briefly. "I'm going to check on Mimi. You okay here?"

"Of course. Absolutely," he says. What's this guys deal, I thought. "Of course, and absolutely." He watches her go, then turns back to me. "She's incredible. You know that already. But I just—" He stops. "Sorry. I talk too much when I'm nervous."

"It's a wedding. Everyone's nervous." I say. Thankful he's at least self-aware.

Rachel sees the church and calls over to me. "Perfect, Leo. This is perfect."

My mother arrived alone. Navy dress, good earrings, lipstick perfect. She was holding a glass of wine she'd walked off with from the cafe below, which she tends to do. She sees me watching. "It's a wedding, Leo." I didn't say anything.

Mimi arrived at 10:30 with her bridesmaids, all of them soaked despite the car service, her dress spattered with mud at the hem. She saw me outside and stopped.

"Santa Margherita?"

"It was this or the Basilica, and you said no icons."

"I wanted nature, but I get it. Weather kicked us in the teeth. So what are we working with?" She talks like an American football coach sometimes. Thinks it's funny.

She looked at the church suspiciously at first, as if walking into it might cause her to set fire, but her body language seemed to change immediately when she craned her neck to see inside the little church. It's plain, rose-colored portico sitting alone in this tiny piazza, empty in the rain. While below, the Basilica of San Francesco, rising through the mist, the whole valley spreading out beyond it and even in this weather, its grounds crawling with tourists.

"So what's this chick's story? Dimmi," she asked.

So I told her about the greatest saint in Assisi, in my mind. The whole story. The murdered lover, the dog, the public confession, the decades of service, the hospital, the congregation of "little poor ones," the visions, Christ calling her _poverella_—little poor one—and her claiming it as identity. When I finished she said, "She built something."

"She built something."

"From being completely fucked."

"From being completely fucked."

Mimi looked at the church again. "All right. We can work with this." She grinned. "Okay, old man. Let's go." Mimi had always called me old. Frequently suggesting I take it easy since my bones were practically dust. I know this is her way of saying she loves me.

Inside, the church was plain but glowing a warm amber from the candles that they had already arranged along the perimeter. The Brooklyn crew was already there, a rainbow of faces and accents, speaking at least three languages, none of them Italian. Mimi walked herself down the aisle. No one giving her away. She'd been clear about that. "Listen, Papa. I'm not property. I'm walking to my own future. This is how we're doing it. Besides, you might trip and break your hip."

Rachel and I sat together in the front row. Edison sat on her other side, quiet now, hands folded. Twenty-eight years. We grew up together. Made three humans before the world became a place where having a kid seems like a crime. I still love her. Differently now, maybe.

She seemed more at peace than I'd seen her in years. Edison's hand rested lightly on the pew between them, not touching her but present. Available. We kept smiling at each other, Rachel and I. Not the smile of ex-spouses being civil. Something else. The smile of people who know each other completely and have stopped pretending otherwise.

Two alphas trying to eat each other alive—that was us. But Edison follows where she leads. He's rich, smart, and pays the tab, but he's her backing band, and I can see this suits her.

The ceremony was short. Declan read a poem—Hopkins, "As Kingfishers Catch Fire"—his voice shaking but steady. I wanted to hug him right then.

Mimi read a short vow she'd written herself that echoed Declan's poem. It was about how marriage and love isn't about completion—she didn't need him to complete her, that's "patriarchal horseshit"—but about recognition. About seeing someone's pattern clearly and choosing to let your patterns intersect. "Like mycelium networks," she said, looking at Declan. "Separate organisms that share resources underground. Nobody sees it happening but the whole forest depends on it. That's what I want. The underground work. The unglamorous symbiosis. You get my weird and I get yours and we just... keep showing up."

Pearl and Walt were nodding, hooked at _mycelium_. They'd given me a book about how trees talk to each other for my birthday last year—_The Hidden Life of Trees_. The three of them spent every vacation hiking, camping, talking about root systems and forest networks. All Eaglescouts, except pearl and she's getting close.

Rachel looked at me after this as if to say, all your fault. The way she thinks. But that was pure Mimi—taking something precious and refusing to let it be precious, stripping away the performance to get at the structure underneath.

The priest said something about marriage being like—and I'm paraphrasing—the mutualism between organisms, each giving what the other needs to survive, and Declan laughed out loud, delighted, because of course this priest had only fifteen minutes to research them, pulled it off perfectly anyway.

Then the priest did something I wasn't expecting. He produced a wine glass wrapped in a white cloth napkin, placed it on the stone floor near the altar.

"From your tradition," he said to Mimi. "The breaking of the glass. It reminds us that even in joy, we remember brokenness. That some things, once shattered, can't be made whole again—but their fragments can still catch light."

Mimi looked at me, eyes wide. Neither of us had asked for this. The priest had done this himself.

I felt a chest pang. Guilt maybe. All my easy contempt for priests, for clergy, for the whole apparatus of organized religion—and here was this kid barely thirty who'd taken the time, who'd understood, who'd brought a wine glass to a Catholic church because he wanted to honor us.

The priest nodded at Declan, who was confused at first, then understood. He stomped on the glass. The crack echoed in the small church.

"Mazel tov!" someone shouted from the back.

Mimi's face—I can't describe it. Not quite smiling. Something deeper. She'd been bat mitzvahed at thirteen, learned enough Hebrew to chant her Torah portion then promptly forgot most of it. Not religious. But this—the glass, the sound, the tradition—she'd claimed it anyway. Built something from fragments.

The rain had stopped. Not gradually—just stopped. The sky still gray but the clouds breaking up, light coming through. The stones wet and shining. Behind the church, the Duomo suddenly visible, the whole cathedral framed perfectly against the valley.

"Photos!" someone shouted. The photographer—someone Rachel hired, a professional from Rome—started organizing people. The wedding party on the steps. Mimi and Declan in front of the church door. The piazza is tiny—maybe seven parking spots worth of space, forming a half-circle in front of the church. A low stone wall curves along the edge, overlooking olive trees that drop down the hillside. Below, the Basilica of San Francesco rises from the valley, its stone layers ascending toward us, the whole valley spreading beyond.

Mimi grabbed my hand, pulled me next to her for a photo. Declan and Rachel on her other side. The four of us in front of Santa Margherita with the Basilica behind us.

The photographer clicked. Mimi looked back at the view, at the composition, at the way the light was breaking through, at the cathedral framed like a Renaissance painting and gave me big hug eyes and that was all I wanted, or ever wanted.

Elliott, the biggest procsuitto in umbria started humming Hava Nagila. Quietly at first, then louder, grinning as he offered his hand to Pearl. She laughed, took it, and they began to dance—Elliott unsurprisingly graceful, Pearl following his lead on the wet stones.

"I'm a New Yorker darling. This isn't my first rodeo."

Rachel and I and Mimi and Walt and Pearl—we have Bruno architecture. That life we created, those decades, those three humans—it just _is_. Inviolable. Memory is the trap because it's not the now. But the architecture? The structure? That's permanent.

The photographer called everyone back. The light was getting better, gold breaking through the gray, the kind of light you can't plan for, can't buy. We stood on the steps of Santa Margherita—the church for the compromised, the ones who built beauty from wreckage—and smiled for the camera.

I thought about reliquaries. My mind in my daughter. The way she sees connections, understands that Margaret of Cortona matters because she started from shame.

Maybe that's what Francis understood. Not poverty as deprivation but as the refusal to be fixed—in place, in identity, in the stories we tell. _Ju-shing woo-chahng_—all conditioned things are impermanent. Francis walked away from his father's house and never built another. Kept descending. Kept choosing the margin.

I'm not home here. I am comfortably alienated. Standing on these steps watching my daughter married, my ex-wife with a kind man, my youngest and my best friend singing and dancing to Hava Nagila in a Catholic piazza—this makes sense not as home but as permanent liminality. The threshold as destination.

The photographer said, "One more!"

We all smiled.

The light held.

## CURATOR'S INTRODUCTION

Olivia Bruno occupies a singular position in contemporary art: a painter whose technical mastery of eighteenth-century Venetian techniques serves not nostalgia but revenge. Trained at the Accademia di Belle Arti in Venice under Professor Marco Benedetti (himself a student of Pietro Annigoni), Bruno brought to her work a vocabulary of celestial pinks, powder blues, and architectural gold that would have been at home in any Venetian palazzo—and then deployed that vocabulary to paint scenes that would have gotten her burned at the stake.

But unlike most artists working in the mode of historical subversion, Bruno wasn't playing with ideas. She was painting what she'd survived. Born into violence—a father who ran illegal gambling, a mother forced into prostitution after he left, a childhood spent raising siblings in a house under a Kansas City viaduct—Bruno learned early that beauty and brutality weren't opposites. They were the same thing viewed from different angles. The men who hurt her thought they were entitled. The paintings say: let's see how that works out.

Her work operates on what I call "trap-door logic": the viewer enters through the front door of formal beauty—that particular Tiepolo luminosity, those Fragonard brushstrokes, that Caravaggio chiaroscuro—only to discover, seconds or minutes later, that the floor has given way beneath them. What appeared to be a Rococo ascension is a castration. What seemed to be divine rapture is premeditated homicide. The technical perfection remains constant; it's the content that shifts.

Bruno's subjects—what she termed her "correction paintings"—take canonical scenes from Western art history and reverse the power dynamics while maintaining the visual vocabulary. In her hands, the male gaze doesn't simply get deconstructed; it gets eviscerated with Baroque precision. The women in her paintings have Würzburg ceiling saint faces while reaching for groins with blades that catch the light like halos.

But here's what makes the work unbearable: these women are genuinely turned on. They're not cold. They're not mechanical. They're aroused. Bruno paints female desire—wet, hungry, fully engaged—and then reveals that desire includes violence. The fairy with her face between another fairy's legs isn't performing duty; she's having the time of her life. The woman drowning a man in the baptismal font looks beatific because she feels beatific. The trap isn't that women are secretly violent. The trap is that women can be horny and homicidal simultaneously, and Western art has spent five hundred years pretending this is impossible.

Bruno is genuinely, devastatingly funny. The centaur getting a pedicure while three women saw through his hooves. The lecher floating in bliss while mermaids with rows of teeth prepare to close their collective mouth. The businessman in the hotel room who doesn't see the child in the corner with the knife. Bruno paints male obliviousness with such tenderness, such technical devotion, that you almost feel bad for them. Almost.

What makes Bruno's work so disorienting—and so necessary—is that she refuses to abandon beauty. These paintings are gorgeous. You could hang them in the Residenz Würzburg and visitors would spend twenty minutes admiring the glazing technique before registering what they're actually seeing. This is not irony. This is Bruno's core argument: that women's rage, women's revenge, women's violence deserves the same painterly devotion, the same formal beauty, that Western art has lavished on male violence for millennia.

She painted like Tiepolo. She thought like someone who'd survived what shouldn't be survivable. She worked like a forger and exhibited like a prophet. This retrospective presents twelve works from across her career—paintings that were, variously, rejected from galleries, removed from exhibitions after complaints, hidden in storage for decades, and in one case, purchased by a collector who had to sign a legal agreement acknowledging he understood what he was buying.

After her Spoleto exhibition was shut down in 2025, a New York Times feature made her internationally famous overnight. She had solo shows in New York, Los Angeles, London, Berlin. She attended none of them. She stayed in her studio in Santa Maria degli Angeli and kept painting while the art world argued about her.

Olivia Bruno died in 2029 at age seventy-five. This exhibition is dedicated to her memory and to her argument: that beauty is not the opposite of violence. Beauty is how violence becomes permanent. And sometimes, beauty is how survival becomes art.

— Dr. Eleanor Whitmore
Curator of Contemporary Art

## ARTIST BIOGRAPHY

**Olivia Maria Bruno** (1954-2029) ARTIST BIOGRAPHY

Olivia Maria Bruno (1954-2029) was born in Independence, Missouri, to an Italian father who ran an illegal gambling operation and an Irish mother from the roughest neighborhood in Kansas City. Her father, Bob Bruno, abandoned the family when Olivia was eleven. Her mother turned to prostitution to survive. Olivia raised her four younger siblings in a house under the viaduct in the River Bottoms while teaching herself to paint from library books.
At sixteen, she gave birth to her son Leo and married his father - her first husband, a jealous man who beat her. It lasted one year. She finished high school anyway, lining every room she lived in with art books stolen or borrowed, painting constantly between shifts at whatever job would take her.

Over the next decades, she would marry four more times - each husband an addict, each marriage a small violence. One beat her with his wooden leg while Leo, sleeping on the couch in their Section 8 duplex, listened through headphones. She kept painting. Through everything, she kept painting.

In 1972, she somehow convinced her way into the Accademia di Belle Arti in Venice, studying under Professor Marco Benedetti, himself a student of Pietro Annigoni. She spent five years learning the Venetian method - build light on light, glaze on glaze - and was expelled twice for fighting with critics about Tiepolo's treatment of women. Both times Benedetti had her reinstated. She returned to Kansas City in 1977 with perfect technique and nowhere to use it.

For thirty-seven years she worked commercial jobs - muralists painting nursery clouds, restaurant Tuscan landscapes, dog portraits in the style of Gainsborough - and painted her own work at night. Thousands of canvases in storage units, in basements, in friends' attics. The "correction paintings" existed as a thread through everything, but she showed almost nothing publicly. When pressed about why, she would say: "I'm not ready. Or they're not ready. One of us isn't ready."

Through her grandmother - Bob Bruno's mother, who spoke Italian and was the only light in that family - Olivia and Leo eventually claimed Italian citizenship. In her late sixties, Olivia married for a sixth time, to a wealthy Italian she met at church. He bought her a loft in Santa Maria degli Angeli near the basilica. She painted there for her final years.

Her first major exhibition took place in Spoleto in 2025 at age 71, curated by Elliott Marsh. It caused immediate controversy and was shut down after three days due to obscenity complaints. A New York Times reporter who'd heard about the closure flew to Spoleto, photographed the work before it was packed away, and published a feature with full-color images. The article went viral. Overnight, Olivia Bruno became famous.

Galleries in New York, Los Angeles, London, and Berlin offered her solo exhibitions. She accepted them all and attended none of them. She stayed in her studio in Santa Maria degli Angeli and kept painting while the art world argued about whether her work was feminist masterpiece or obscene trash. She didn't care. She'd already survived worse arguments.

On March 18, 2029, Leo found his mother in her studio in Santa Maria degli Angeli. The space heater had been running all night - the old kind that burns kerosene, the kind she'd bought at a flea market because it reminded her of her grandmother's home. The windows were shut tight against the March cold. The room was warm. Too warm.

She was sitting in her chair facing the easel. Still wrapped in her paint-stained cardigan. Looking at _The Barber's Chair_ but not seeing it anymore. She'd gone still the way things go still when they're done with moving.

The painting was unfinished. The man's face complete, the razor complete, the mirror complete. But the women's bodies ended at the shoulders. Their faces were blank canvas.

In her notebook beside the easel, a single line in increasingly unsteady handwriting: "The razor hasn't touched his throat yet."

Her estate was left to her son Leo, who has refused all interviews and declined to authorize a biography. When asked by a journalist what he thought his mother's work meant, he said only: "She painted what she survived. Whether anyone wanted to see it or not."

This is, as far as can be determined, accurate.

What's devastating about this painting is how beautiful it is. Bruno has used every trick Tiepolo knew: that light-on-light glazing that makes flesh glow, the way fabric becomes transparent at the waterline, the particular pink of pleasure that reads as both erotic and divine. She wants you seduced. She wants you complicit. And then she wants you to see the teeth.

### Plate 11: _The Pedicure_

Oil on canvas, 150 × 200 cm, 2018  
Collection of Dr. Eleanor Whitmore

A centaur getting a pedicure. His horse-half on a fainting couch, his human-half reading a newspaper. Three women in togas attend to his hooves—filing, buffing, painting them gold. The whole scene is pure Pompeian fresco luxury: rose marble columns, that particular powder-blue sky, the kind of decadent leisure that suggests empire at its peak.

His face: absolute contentment. The newspaper blocks his view. The headline is visible: "Missing Persons Report Delayed."

But look at what the women are actually doing. The file is a saw. The gold paint is molten. And they're not looking at his hooves. They're looking at each other. Timing something.

Bruno has painted this with complete Rococo frivolity—the brushwork is loose, sensual, suggesting pleasure and ease. The togas are rendered with that particular Fragonard fabric-as-air quality. Everything about the technique says: relax. This is a scene of luxury and care.

Until you notice the saw. Until you see the women's faces. Until you read the headline.

The painting hung in Bruno's studio for two years before she sold it. When asked why she kept it so long, she said: "I liked looking at his face. The before-face. The face that doesn't know yet."

### Plate 12: _The Barber's Chair_

Oil on canvas, 180 × 220 cm, 2029 (unfinished)  
Collection of the Artist's Estate

A man sits in a barber's chair. Three women attend him. One lathers his face with the kind of careful attention Vermeer gave to domestic labor. One sharpens the razor—that particular Caravaggio light catching the blade, making it a focal point, making it holy. The third holds a mirror so he can watch.

The whole composition is structured like a Dutch guild portrait: every tool rendered with devotional precision, everyone in their proper station, the painting celebrating the dignity of craft.

His face: relaxed. Eyes closed. Trusting. That particular vulnerability of male grooming, when men put themselves in women's hands and assume good faith.

But look at the reflection in the mirror. It shows something different. Not the shop. A room full of photographs. Polaroids pinned to every surface. Children. The woman holding the mirror isn't showing him his reflection. She's showing him his archive. She's showing him what she knows.

And her face—catching the light like a Vermeer, painted with that particular luminosity that suggests divine presence—isn't the attendant anymore. She's the prosecutor. The razor hasn't touched his throat yet.

Bruno left this painting unfinished. The man's face is complete, the razor is complete, the mirror is complete. But the background is underpainting—you can see the grid lines, the compositional structure, the pentimenti where she changed her mind. The women's bodies end at the shoulders. Their faces are blank canvas.

In her notebook beside the easel, a single line: "The razor hasn't touched his throat yet."

She was found dead the next morning.
