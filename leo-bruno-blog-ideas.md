**What the Bus Knows**
Thirty minutes each morning between Quartucciu and Cagliari as a complete theory of everything. The bus is the real seminar room.

**Sardinia Is Not Italy (And It Knows It)**
An island that regards the peninsula the way a cat regards a dog: present, acknowledged, fundamentally untranslatable. Italy the way Kansas is America — technically true, culturally its own planet.

**Cagliari Fell Into the Sea Like a Braque**
The bus turns a corner. A city appears, fractured and cubist, falling apart into the sea. You think: I'm never leaving. Then you leave. On the two true things that can sit in the same bus ride.

**The Autogrill at the End of the Relationship**
A meditation on roadside coffee as existential statement. He's won his stupid symbolic victory and has absolutely nothing to show for it except a too-hot mug of compromise and a borrowed cigarette.

**Flamingos at the Edge of the Industrial Zone**
Molentargius, the longue durée, and why beauty insists on existing next to the ugly. On Braudel, lagoons, and the specific persistence of pink birds.

**Italian Teenage Boys Groom Each Other's Hair on the Bus**
With natural tenderness that would be impossible for American boys of the same age. On what gets lost in translation between cultures, and what Sardinia quietly keeps.

**On Homing: Why This Move Is Different From All the Other Moves**
KC, Georgia, Chapel Hill, Durham, Dallas, Austin, KC, Des Moines, Italy. On the difference between restlessness and homing instinct, and how you finally know which one is operating.

**What Does It Mean That I'm Writing 1212 Arezzo From an Island That Has No Memory of It?**
On displacement, imagination, and why the stones eventually require your physical presence. Not mysticism. Just: the light.

**The Funicolare Stops at Nine**
Montecatini Alto, winter, no supermarket. On the seductiveness of small beautiful inconvenient places and what they're actually offering you.

## SUGAR CREEK / THE AMERICAN PAST / GERONIMO

**The Good Father and the Bad Father Are the Same Man**
On Doug: drug dealer, baseball coach, Leavenworth for ten years. Still alive. Still wearing the cowboy hat and leather driving gloves. A Father Karamazov in the American Midwest who is basically Mickey Rourke.

**Tangled Up in Blue Was the Last Song I Remember**
A Dylan concert, 1994, a carpeted van with a table in the back, valium and beer before the show. A strip club I never wanted to be in. On the mythology of the American hippie from someone who was actually in the room. These were the people who went to the concerts. Fought in Vietnam. Hated Nixon. And slapped their old ladies around.

**What the Stew Meant**
Independence, Missouri, 1994. Doug's drug house. A spiral staircase. John Prine on the stereo. A pot of stew on the stove. The house quiet for once. On love between men who can't say it, and the specific tenderness of a meal in a bad house.

**Grandmother DeLuca Saved My Life (She Didn't Know It Either)**
Ozarks. Cantaloupe. Cicadas. Fishing. Books. Art. She taught me: beauty exists in the same place as everything else — not despite, not because. Just: in the same place. On the inheritance that splits clean down the middle.

**The Osage River Remembers What Missouri Forgot**
On fishing with my grandfather on water that used to be the center of someone else's world. The name survives. The people become ghosts. The land keeps its secrets. On how American memory works: Wazhazhe, the Children of the Middle Waters, and two men fishing without talking about any of it.

**I Don't Say Any of It**
On carrying a whole brutal catalog into every room you enter — fifteen houses, five stepfathers, Sugar Creek in the 1970s, men in their freest state — and what you do when someone asks about your childhood and the honest answer takes two hours and ruins dinner.

**Geronimo**
The man who fought an impossible war. Turned into a word. Soldiers yell it jumping out of airplanes. On what America does with its enemies: kills the man, keeps the name, makes it mean whatever is convenient. _[Seed — let it be short.]_

## MUSIC

**Eddie Cochran to David Bowie: How a Riff Becomes a Cathedral**
On the body's knowledge, the three-chord transmission, and what Little Steven's Underground Garage radio station has to do with the immortal soul. The same opening riff. Fifty years apart. The blood just knows.

**The Body Interrupts the Composer**
Why Bowie's "oh yeah" and Prince's grunt are more honest than anything Radiohead ever planned. On the Dionysian interruption of the Apollonian — the moment the body takes the pen away from the mind and writes something true.

**Why I Play Kendrick Lamar at 7am Before Talking to Anyone**
A spiritual defense of "King Kunta" as morning liturgy. On holding space vs. flowing through, and the music that tells you which one you are before you've had coffee.

I wake up at 7:23 in the morning and my heart feels like a cage full of baby eagles all trying to screech out a joe cocker song. spastic, halting, full of weird pathos. i still dream. that's part of the problem. and i'm old and my stomach is a pit of demons. probably the reason for the dreams. i these dreams - they bleed into my morning and i spend some very unpleasant minutes trying to find the key for cage in my chest and it's never there. but by the time i hit the shower at 8:30 I've found the key and my song is King Kunta.

**Sturgill Simpson, Paul Westerberg, and the Theology of the Almost-Famous**
On genius that refuses to cooperate with its own success, and what that costs. The artists who are constitutionally incapable of giving you what you need to make them famous.

**Justin Townes Earle Walked Into a Bar in Austin**
A tribute in the form of a true story, mostly. On what it means to name your son after a man who was named after a man, and the specific weight of that chain.

**The Channel That Stays Open: On Prince and Dylan and the Artists Who Can't Stop**
On geniuses who produce terrible work because they're operating as open pipes without quality filters, and why that's the only honest way to work. Bob Dylan recording seventeen albums you'd never listen to is the price of "Tangled Up in Blue."

**Uncle Tupelo at the Folly Theater, Kansas City, 1993**
Beck. Alejandro Escovedo. On seeing the future of American music in small rooms before anyone knew it was the future, and what happens to that feeling thirty years later.

**Mötley Crüe, Dostoevsky, and the Case for the Honest Body**
Tommy Lee over Galileo. This is an actual philosophical position. On choosing sentimentality over cynicism as an intellectual act, accepting Christ without faith, and why the honest body is more trustworthy than performed depth.

**Bowie Is a Temple Full of Wolves**
On the synthesis of structure and wildness. The artist as architecture you can live inside. What it means when a record feels like a place you actually went.

**How to Write a Saint**

## ART / PAINTING / OLIVIA

**My Mother Painted Dirty Tiepolos in a Kansas City Basement for 37 Years**
On Olivia Bruno, self-taught painter, dead before I could ask her how she learned to make flesh glow from library books. On what it means to try to finish someone else's testimony. On painting as exorcism.

**I'm Not Trying to Become a Painter. I'm Trying to Finish the Work.**
The Florence atelier application I will write someday. No bullshit about "interrogating the relationship between." Just: she painted this. I wrote about it. Now I need to know what she knew. Help. _[They'll take you.]_

**The Glow From Within**
How Venetian glazing works and why it matters. Light passes through the glazes, bounces off the ground, comes back up. You're not painting light — you're building a structure that catches it. On why this is also how memory works, and how fiction works, and how certain relationships work.

**Small Studies**
On starting to paint again at 56. The blank canvases stacked in the corner. The specific humility of beginning. On Olivia and what she knew that I don't yet. _[Could be a recurring series — one painting, one piece of writing, as the practice develops.]_

## PHILOSOPHY / POLITICS / CULTURE

**Figure Skating Judging and the Rage of the Ruled**
Why we keep having this meltdown every four years, and what it actually means. On the tension between subjective and objective evaluation systems, and why skating controversies are a perfect microcosm for how merit, connections, and institutional power operate in society.

**The Lutnick on the Stoop**
Jon Stewart, Howard Lutnick, and the word "trapezium" as a complete theory of American power. On class performance, the gaps between what people perform and what they actually do, and why the malapropism is always the tell.

**Elon Musk Sent That Email on Christmas Day**
On comedic architecture, the strategic use of revealing details, and what it means that this is how we process catastrophe now — through a guy with good timing standing behind a desk.

**America, Viewed from a Bus in Sardinia**
On distance as a diagnostic tool. The rattlesnakes. The walls. The subdivisions named after what they destroyed. On what you see when you're four thousand miles away and the news arrives on your phone at 7am.

**Accepting Christ Without Faith: The Case for Sentimentality**
Dostoevsky, The Idiot, and why cynicism is just cowardice in a tuxedo. On choosing sentimentality as an intellectual position, not a failure of rigor. On the Buddha and Tommy Lee and what they have in common.

**In Defense of Mama's Boys**
_Tags: Culture, Italy, Satire, America, Family_
On the American pathologizing of the Italian son who calls his mother every day — the Freudian scarlet letter, the therapy-speak about "enmeshment" — as itself the pathology. The 45-year-old in Cagliari who still has his laundry done and his Sunday lunch waiting and nobody thinks twice about it, versus the American guy who did "the work" and now eats sad salads alone and calls it independence. At 25, choosing your mother over your girlfriend is avoidance. At 56, it might just be clarity — you've done the math on relationships and you know what holds. The mammismo mockery always comes from cultures that abandoned their elderly to institutions and then wrote self-help books about healthy boundaries. The man who's "worked through" his mother stuff in therapy and sees her twice a year at Thanksgiving is not healthier. He's just lonelier and has better language for it. Funny the whole way down, then one true sentence at the bottom: the specific texture of loving someone who is also diminishing. Walking her down from church. Tending the wound that won't heal. That's not enmeshment. That's just love in its least glamorous and most true form. The Sedaris move: reader thinks they're reading a comedy, then realizes they've been reading something else entirely.

**Things That Make Me Want to Hurl: A Catalogue of People Living Their Best Life**
_Tags: Culture, Satire, Wellness, America_
On the specific nausea induced by the wellness-industrial complex and its foot soldiers. The cold plunge guys with their missionary energy — that mix of pity and urgency, like you're dying and the cure is being cold and miserable at 5am. People who are "intentional" about everything, as if the rest of us are just accidentally eating lunch. Anyone with "boundaries" who means "preferences." The word "journey," especially when the journey is learning to like vegetables. People who quit drinking and need you to know about it the way evangelicals need you to know about Jesus. Gratitude journals — the journaling of the gratitude specifically, as if God is up there going: _well, she wrote it down, so it counts._ Linen. Not the fabric — the identity. The person who has become linen. "I've done a lot of work on myself," deployed as a get-out-of-jail-free card for current behavior. Meditation apps that send you notifications, the irony invisible to everyone involved. "Abundance mindset" — the mindset of someone who has abundance and would like credit for their attitude about it. Going minimalist while still owning a car and a spare bedroom for arts and crafts. Anyone whose dog is their "anchor." The person who found a great therapist and now recommends therapy to everyone who expresses any emotion including mild inconvenience. This one writes itself. Open with "people living their best life" and let it run. Sedaris would be proud.

start with: two of my most beloved friends are in AA and both talk nonstop about gratitude. And it triggers me. I imagine taking them both to a carnival and have them find their best life in a tub of water full of apples. And then when they start to bob and chomp just hold them under. For their sake, more than mine.

## THE DUNCERY CROSSES THE AISLE

Subtitle: Anatomy of a Book Ban
A New Jersey school district bans Junot Díaz's Pulitzer-winning novel over "mental health concerns." The book is about what happens to kids when adults refuse to tell the truth about violence, sex, and history. The ban is the thing the book warned you about.
Key argument: book-banning isn't ideological, it's parental — which means it's bipartisan. The MAGA parent and the progressive parent are pulling the same rope from opposite ends. MAGA pulls Oscar Wao for darkness and sex. The progressive pulls Mockingbird for the n-word in a white author's mouth. Both mistake their own discomfort for their child's damage.
The irony: a book about a kid destroyed by silence, banned for breaking it.
The cast: the school board meeting. The parent at the microphone who hasn't read it. The administrator who has and is nodding anyway. The teacher in the back looking at her hands who taught it for eleven years.
Possible form: the Trump one-act, but without Trump. Just the room. Just the Americans in it.
Closing line candidate: America doesn't ban books because it's evil. It bans books because it's afraid. Evil has at least made a decision.

Both shows will absolutely make sure you get it. But in opposite ways. The Sopranos beats you over the head with symbols — the ducks, Tony's coma dreams, that fucking priest quoting the Stones without attribution like we're all supposed to nod knowingly. It's operatic heavy-handedness. Baroque. You're watching a prestige show do prestige things.
The Pitt is heavy-handed in the other direction: it explains. It doesn't trust you to understand what emergency medicine actually is, so it'll have someone spell it out. A character will basically turn to camera and say "this is what's broken about the healthcare system" instead of just letting you watch it break.
But here's the thing: both work anyway. The Sopranos earns its obviousness through sheer dramatic weight. The Pitt earns it because the message — about what we've done to emergency rooms, to nurses, to the whole apparatus — is too important to trust to subtext.
And the single-day structure does something else entirely. It's not compressing time like normal TV — it's expanding it. You're living the shift. Feeling the accumulation. The way one bad decision compounds into another. That's closer to theater than television. Closer to real time than narrative time.
The heavy-handedness becomes the point: this is heavy. It should feel heavy.

BREAKING BAD HAD THE MOST DISCIPLINED WRITING AND I CAN PROVE IT
Every scene pointing somewhere. Nothing wasted. No prestige meander. No "let's do a bottle episode because the actor wanted to try something." Just: cause, effect, escalation, consequence.
Compare that to The Sopranos, which I love — but Tony will be a genius about human nature one episode and a complete fucking moron the next, depending on what the plot needs. Christopher never coheres as a person. He's a collection of traits the writers bolt on season by season: Hollywood dreams, addiction, violence, weird sensitivity. When they need him to do something, he does it. When they don't, he mopes.
The Bear has the opposite problem: it over-psychologizes. That last season got so deep into Carmy's specific damage that the whole thing ran aground. Like, I get it. I could've helped them cover more ground without losing the depth.
But Breaking Bad? Walter White makes sense the whole way through. Every decision tracks. Every character operates by their own logic, and the writers never cheat. That's discipline. That's craft.
The Wire is the only thing that comes close, but The Wire is playing a different game — it's Balzac, it's panoramic. Breaking Bad is a knife.

OLD TV WAS JUST STAGE PLAYS WITH CAMERAS AND THAT'S WHY IT HOLDS
Three cameras. Limited sets. The actors holding moments longer because there's nowhere to cut. It forced a different kind of presence.
MASH. All in the Family. The pacing. The silences. That intro music that hits you like ritual. You're not watching television — you're watching people in a room talking, and the room barely changes, so the talking has to do everything.
Norman Lear came out of theater. Emerson College, scholarship for an oratorical contest. His whole career was importing stage discipline into the sitcom. That's why All in the Family feels the way it does — the sets are stagey on purpose. Shot on videotape, live studio audience, no film gloss. You're watching something closer to community theater than prestige TV, and that's the power.
The modern stuff — even the good stuff — is always cutting. Always moving. The camera is doing half the work. Old TV made the actors do it. Made the writing do it.
That's why it's soothing. You know the shape of the thing. The intro is a ritual. The pacing lets you breathe. It's reliable.
And that's also why Virgin River works, by the way. It's honest about being comfort food. No prestige pretensions. Just: here's the shape, here's the ritual, settle in.

FREAKS AND GEEKS IS DEVASTATING IN WAYS THAT SNEAK UP ON YOU
Linda Cardellini's quiet desperation. The way the show understands that adolescence is mostly waiting and not fitting and wanting something you can't name.
It got one season. Probably couldn't have sustained itself much longer — the preciousness would've curdled, the nostalgia would've started performing itself. But that one season is nearly perfect.
The show doesn't do anything. That's the whole thing. High school doesn't do anything either. You're just there. Enduring it. The freaks in the parking lot. The geeks in the gym. Everyone waiting for life to start, and life is just... this.
Most shows about teenagers are about drama. Freaks and Geeks is about the absence of drama. The low hum of everyday humiliation and boredom and the occasional flash of something that feels like meaning but probably isn't.
That's why it sneaks up on you. You think you're watching a comedy. Then you realize you've been watching something else entirely.

THE ADRIANA DEATH SCENE AND WHY THE SOPRANOS IS UNEVEN BUT WHEN IT HITS IT OPERATES AT A LEVEL MOST TELEVISION NEVER APPROACHES
That car ride with Silvio. The length of it. The way the scene just sits there and makes you endure it with her.
What makes it work: Silvio isn't savoring it. He's not conflicted. He's just doing a task. The banality. Adriana pleading, animal terror, crawling through the leaves, and he's already mentally moved on.
Drea de Matteo plays the dissolution of hope in real time. You watch her understand — not all at once but in waves — that there's no argument, no appeal, nothing she can say. The scene earns its length because it makes you sit in that hopelessness.
The show is uneven. Christopher doesn't make sense. Junior speaking Italian and singing opera but being devastated by oral sex gossip — the psychological logic is thin. Tony's intelligence varies by episode depending on what the plot needs.
But when The Sopranos hits — "Long Term Parking," the finale, Adriana's death — it's operating at a level most television never approaches.
That's the thing about uneven shows. The peaks justify the valleys.
