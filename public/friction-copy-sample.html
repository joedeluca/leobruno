Friction is the Business

Original thought leadership essay for Playmoove, a mobile vehicle-sharing platform based in Cagliari, Sardinia. Written to establish the brand's positioning in the B2B fleet-sharing market — arguing that operational complexity isn't a problem to be engineered away, but the source of durable competitive advantage.

Full Text

Dilating on a particularly problematic detail — the minutiae of it — is often the best way to get your arms around the bigger problem. In this case, the big problem is maximizing profit in a mobile sharing operation. The minutiae: rebalancing algorithms.

Call them recipes instead. That's what they are. Instructions: if station A is empty and station B is overflowing, move vehicles from B to A. Simple. Elegant. The kind of thing that works perfectly in a computer programmer's head.

Then you turn the system on.

What the recipe doesn't account for: the driver who won't work Sundays, station B at the bottom of a one-way hill everyone avoids, customers leaving vehicles wherever they want. The recipe assumes a frictionless world. The world has friction.

Here's what actually happens: You deploy your sublime optimization on Monday. By Wednesday, your operations manager has a manual override system scribbled in the margins of a printed map. By Friday, the drivers are ignoring it entirely. By the following Monday, you're in a meeting trying to figure out why your utilization rates are declining even though the dashboard says they should be improving — and your operations manager is talking like a man who's already quit but hasn't told anyone yet.

The math is perfect on paper. Which solves exactly zero real-world problems.

"Every vendor in this space sells optimization as if friction is a problem to solve. But friction isn't a problem — it's the business."

That friction — the particular detail I mentioned at the start — is the thing. Driver availability, vehicle capacity, station accessibility, weather patterns, local events, maintenance windows. These aren't edge cases. They're the operation. A system that can't account for them isn't optimizing your fleet. It's optimizing a theoretical fleet that doesn't exist.

At Playmoove, we don't have to imagine what operational friction looks like. We run our own car-sharing fleet in Cagliari — a city built on hills, flooded with tourists in summer, choked with construction, and governed by a particular kind of friction that could only be described as Italian. We built Playmoove because we needed it. Every constraint in the system is one we've hit ourselves.

Here's what dilating on this rebalancing failure reveals: the profit isn't in the recipe. It's in the friction.

The companies making money in mobile sharing aren't the ones with the most elegant math. They're the ones who understand that the operation is the friction. The money lives in how well you handle the Sunday driver shortage, the one-way hill problem, and the customer who parks wherever he pleases.

Craft Note 01
Opening Rhetorical structure

The dilation move

The essay opens by naming its own method — "dilating on a particularly problematic detail" — which does two things at once: earns the reader's trust by signaling analytical rigor, and frames the rebalancing algorithm as a lens rather than a subject. The technique lets the piece operate at two levels simultaneously: granular enough to be credible, expansive enough to carry a brand argument.

Craft Note 02
Escalation Narrative compression

Monday through Monday

The week-by-week collapse of the optimization system — Monday deploy, Wednesday workaround, Friday mutiny, the following Monday's postmortem — is a compressed narrative arc inside a B2B essay. It earns its specificity. The operations manager "talking like a man who's already quit but hasn't told anyone yet" is the sentence that turns a business argument into something the reader has lived.

Craft Note 03
Positioning Competitive differentiation

Reframing the category

The strategic move of the essay is the inversion: the industry sells friction as a solvable problem; Playmoove argues it's the source of profit. This reframe lets Playmoove claim a positioning that competitors can't easily copy — you can't say "we also embrace friction" after spending years selling frictionlessness.

The Cagliari passage is load-bearing. "A particular kind of friction that could only be described as Italian" turns operator credibility into personality. It's the moment the company stops being a vendor and becomes a character.

Craft Note 04
Closing Verdict structure

The money lives in

The closing deliberately echoes the opening dilation technique — returning to the Sunday driver, the one-way hill, the customer who parks wherever he pleases. Coming back to the specific after the abstract argument is finished. The final sentence doesn't reach for a grand formulation. It stays granular, which is the point the essay has been making all along.
