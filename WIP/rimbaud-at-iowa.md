# RIMBAUD IN IOWA

FADE IN:

INT. IOWA WRITERS' WORKSHOP - SEMINAR ROOM - DAY

A circle of earnest MFA students. Coffee cups. Moleskines. One person has a therapy dog. A whiteboard reads: "WORKSHOP GUIDELINES — RESPECT THE WORK."

RIMBAUD (17, unwashed, drunk at 2pm, smells like absinthe and fuck-you) slouches in his chair. His manuscript pages are covered in wine stains and what might be blood. He is the only person not making eye contact.

WORKSHOP LEADER (50s, tweed skirt, kind smile, dead inside) stands at the whiteboard with a dry-erase marker.

WORKSHOP LEADER
Welcome, everyone. Before we begin, I want to go over some ground rules. These aren't meant to constrain you — they're meant to protect you. And the work.

She writes "THE WORK" on the whiteboard and underlines it twice.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
Rule one. When we discuss a poem, we use craft language. We don't say "I like this" or "I don't like this." We talk about what the poem is doing. What the poem is reaching for.

RIMBAUD pulls a flask from his coat. Drinks. No one acknowledges this.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
Rule two. When a poem is being workshopped, the author is silent.

She lets this land. Looks around the circle.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
You don't defend. You don't explain. You sit. And you listen. This can be uncomfortable. That's okay.

RIMBAUD snorts. The therapy dog looks at him.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
Rule three. We refer to "the speaker," not "you." The poem is its own object. We say "the speaker feels" not "you feel." This protects the author. It also protects the poem.

She writes "THE SPEAKER ≠ THE AUTHOR" on the whiteboard.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
Rule four — and this is important — imagery must be earned. If the poem makes a big emotional move, we ask: has the poem done the work to get us there? Has it earned that moment?

RIMBAUD
(to no one, quietly)
Earned.

WORKSHOP LEADER
Arthur?

RIMBAUD
(still looking at the window)
Nothing. Go on. Tell us what the poem wants.

WORKSHOP LEADER
(beat, then recovering)
Rule five. We don't offer solutions. If something isn't working, we name it. We don't rewrite each other's poems. We say "this moment felt unearned" not "you should change this line to —"

RIMBAUD shifts in his chair. He is now visibly restless, like something chemical is happening.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
And finally — we lead with generosity. What's working? What has energy? Then we move to concerns. It's a conversation. Not a trial.

She caps the marker. Smiles.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
Okay. So. Let's start with Marcus's poem today. Marcus, you know the rules — just listen.

MARCUS (28, earnest, from Kansas City, has a tattoo of a Rilke quote he won't show anyone) nods solemnly. Folds his hands.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
Who'd like to begin?

STUDENT 1 (cardigan, septum piercing, uses "praxis" unironically)
I just want to say — I really felt the restraint here. The terra cotta image recurs but it never overplays its hand. The poem trusts the reader.

General nodding.

STUDENT 3 (Amherst, has read Bluets four times, once aloud to an ex)
I agree. And "sweetness seeping into root and stone" — that line is doing so much work. It's grounding the whole emotional register of the piece.

WORKSHOP LEADER
Good. What about the ending? "She tells herself this is enough."

STUDENT 1
I think it's earned.

More nodding. RIMBAUD shifts in his chair. Something is happening to him physically.

STUDENT 3
I do wonder if the poem could sit with the image a little longer before the turn. Like — the lemon tree. There's more there. But the restraint is really lovely.

WORKSHOP LEADER
Marcus, I think we're all saying the same thing — this is careful, controlled work. You should feel good about this.

MARCUS looks like he just received communion.

RIMBAUD has stopped listening. He is writing on the back of his own manuscript. Fast, almost violent. The flask is out. The therapy dog has moved to the far side of the room.

No one notices.

The workshop continues. We hear it recede — voices going soft, the language becoming a wash of "tension" and "register" and "earned" — as the CAMERA SLOWLY PUSHES IN on Rimbaud's hand moving across the page.

WORKSHOP LEADER (CONT'D)
(looks at her folder)
Arthur, welcome. We're so glad you're here. Now, I've read your poem "The Drunken Boat" —

RIMBAUD
Le Bateau Ivre.

WORKSHOP LEADER
Yes. And I think there's real... energy here. But I'm wondering if we might workshop some of the imagery? Make it more... accessible?

STUDENT 1
I just want to say I really felt this piece. But I'm troubled by the colonial implications of the boat metaphor—

RIMBAUD
It's not a metaphor.

STUDENT 1
Right, but all language is—

RIMBAUD
IT'S NOT A FUCKING METAPHOR.

(pause)

The boat is drunk. I am the boat. The boat is me drunk on rivers. The rivers are drunk on me. There is no separation. There is only—

WORKSHOP LEADER
Arthur, I hear you. I do. But in workshop, we try not to use "I statements" when giving feedback—

RIMBAUD stands. Buttons his coat (which is filthy).

RIMBAUD
You people are already dead and don't know it.

He walks to the door leaving his pages on the desk. Stops. Turns.

RIMBAUD (CONT'D)
"Luminous" or "luminescent?" When you decide in 20 years, remember: I chose the desert.

He exits.

STUDENT 2 (earnest, from Connecticut, working on a novel about his grandmother's curio cabinet)
Should we... should we still workshop his sestina on Thursday?

WORKSHOP LEADER
(sipping coffee, already exhausted)
Yes, Marcus. We'll workshop Arthur's sestina on Thursday.

CUT TO:

INT. SEMINAR ROOM — LATER

The room is empty. Chairs pushed back. Coffee cups abandoned.

On Rimbaud's desk: his pages, wine-stained, half-illegible. And beneath them, on the back of Marcus's poem, in Rimbaud's handwriting:

CLOSE ON THE PAGE:

_A glass of water in the night,_
_summer heat wafting across cool terra cotta, barefoot_
_after they made love in the kitchen,_
_the cool meeting the beads of sweat on her back._

_The one cat slow blinking at the piccione cucciolo,_
_waiting for the fat kerplunk._
_The lemon tree he planted in 1969._
_The thing that writhes and hisses in the dark._

_He breaks the tap on the nectary_
_with simple gravity._

The WORKSHOP LEADER stands over the desk. She picks up the page.

She reads it.

She reads it again.

Something crosses her face that hasn't been there in a long time. Not approval. Not the workshop smile. Something older than that. Something she went to graduate school to feel and then spent twenty years teaching herself not to.

She sets the page down carefully, as if it might still be alive.

She looks at the empty chair where he sat.

But Arthur never comes back.

Twenty years later, the workshop leader is still there, still kind, still dead inside.

Arthur is dead too. But he died in Marseille with one leg, having lived more in 37 years than all of them will live in 80.

FADE TO BLACK.

## Notes

The classic workshop rules for discussing poems:

1. Craft terms.
   No "I like" or "I don't like" — you're supposed to talk about what the poem "is doing" or "trying to do," not your personal taste. Everything gets framed in craft terms.

2. Author is silent.
   The author stays silent — the poet whose work is being workshopped can't speak, defend, or explain. They just sit there and take notes. (Imagine Rimbaud with that rule.)

3. The speaker. Not you.
   Refer to "the speaker," not "you" — you never say "you wrote" or "you feel," you say "the speaker does X." The polite fiction that the poem is a separate artifact from the person who made it.

4. The poem as little creature.
   Use "the poem" as the subject — "The poem wants to…" "The poem is reaching for…" "The poem hasn't yet earned…" The poem becomes this autonomous little creature with its own desires.

5. All is earned, or not.
   "Earned" and "unearned" — maybe the most MFA word of all. An image, a turn, an emotion has to be "earned." Nothing is just allowed to happen.

6. No solutions. Just ID problems.
   No prescriptive feedback — you're not supposed to say "you should change this line to X." You identify problems, you don't offer solutions. (In practice everyone violates this constantly.)
   Positive before negative, or the "sandwich" method — say something good first, then your concerns, then something good again.
   Avoid "I think what you're trying to say is…" — you're not supposed to rewrite the poet's intention for them.

7. Common vocabulary
   Common vocabulary that floats around: "tension," "stakes," "landing," "register," "move," "gesture," "received language," "the turn," "compression," "earned," "doing work."
