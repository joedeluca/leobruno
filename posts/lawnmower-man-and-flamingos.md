## What He Sees

We cross under the cloverleaf overpass and emerge with a long glimpse of the estuary—thousands of nesting flamingos, and above, the distinct arrow shape of one in flight.

Then: used car dealership, glass repair, IKEA, beer-and-burger joint, Chinese buffet that packs them in all week. Into the heart of the old city, the monuments, the tourists. My workday begins.

But what did the lawnmower man see?

## The Rhythms of the Little Quarter

I work in the center of a medium-sized city in Sardegna. I ride the bus back and forth from my apartment in a little quarter on the perimeter. The sea is walking distance, though it's an unpleasant walk—traffic, dilapidated sidewalks or none at all.

Tonight I stop at the grocery chain. Buy broccoli and tater tots for dinner. On the way back I avoid Elena, who has a fruit and vegetable shop, because I don't want her to see I've bought broccoli from the chain.

So I slink down a side street, feeling deflated. I'd been relying on her greeting more as the nights have grown longer. I arrive in the quarter in the dark now. Her shop—the size of a large American bathroom—is packed with perfect fruits and vegetables and eggs from her chickens.

It's a warm, colorful antidote to the rains and darkness of November. Though Elena has a world of complaints about prices and her decaying hips and knees, she's alive and present. If you have one of those resilient minds capable of using the word "happy," you'd say she is.

## The Bubble Tea Girl

The bubble tea girl smokes a lot, stooped against her shop window on the sidewalk. I pass within inches of her each evening. She's always looking down at her phone. No interest in passers-by. No interest in bubble tea either, as far as I can tell.

I've never seen a customer inside. Never seen her do anything but smoke or scroll.

Unless her friends visit. Then they stand in the center of the overly-lit shop, tightly grouped like a murder of crows, all dressed in black, surrounding the bubble tea girl who appears as a tropical bird in her bubble tea hat and pink-and-white uniform—like she just floated down from bubble tea heaven into a world full of crows.

## Sunday Laundry

It's Sunday afternoon. I'm late getting my linens on the line. In summer they'd dry in an hour, but it's December now. Cool, clear, bright—like almost every day for weeks. Perpetual Indian summer.

I'm wearing wool slippers and two sets of pajamas as I pin sheets on the line that crisscrosses my front balcony. The sheet grazes my basil plants. The aroma wafts up, mingling with fresh linen. My neighbor keeps beer on his porch. He's out, shirtless, drinking a beer, staring at the lemon tree next door that's bursting with fruit.

My apartment has no heating or cooling system. My system is simple: layers of clothes that correspond to the seasons and time of day.

A very old man lives directly across from me. We do our laundry on Sundays. I know his wardrobe. Invariably one of his black socks falls into the adjoining courtyard. I have to put a rock from the ficus into it to toss it back up to his balcony. When it hits, I hear him say "grazie."

## The Lady Who Woke Up Somewhere

The number 30 was packed. The usual routine of inching from back to front as more people board. I find a lady asleep, the seat beside her free. I tap her shoulder. No response. Again, a little firmer. She startles, eyes opening wide, scanning around quickly like she's woken somewhere unexpected.

She doesn't move. Looks at me. I point to the seat. "Me, there?" She doesn't seem to understand. Then it clicks. She gets up. I move to the window seat.

She's very small. Stands in the aisle scanning the world outside as it passes. She's established that we're on a bus. Where it is, she doesn't know.

## Hyper-Realism as Antidote

Do we look backwards? What value is there in looking backwards?

Answer: no. We don't look back explicitly. It's a statement in itself, to never look back.

Streetlights and shop lights that line the street splinter in the wet night. Bus windows fog around the edges. It's warm in the back because the only door opening is at the front—a ploy the driver uses to inspect each passenger for a validated ticket. To leave the bus you have to squeeze past passengers standing in every available space.

I cannot make it back to front in time, so I move forward a little at a time.

## The Inchness of Things

It's easy to lump all passengers together in your mind, but they're as unlike as any other random collection of citizens. Some rich, others poor. Africans, Indians, tourists. School kids going to school in the morning with the workers.

You can tell who lives alone, or isn't closely attended to: crimped collar, hair standing up in back, a missed belt loop.

I'm stunned by an old woman with thinning hair. She smiles straight ahead at the young girls in front of her. They're like otters, nestled around one of their telephones, enraptured by the stream. The boys who gave up their seats so she could sit stand uncomfortably close to the door. This is best for us all. We're all more comfortable for their small sacrifice.

If you're going to be scolded or directed to do something on a city bus, it's most likely by a woman over 40. These are the ones who rule the island. They maintain order at home, at work, on the bus.

This is a remnant of the old life when men spent their days in fields with sheep and goats, and women ran everything else.

## What the Men Talk About

In the evening, old men gather on benches around the center where they talk incessantly about things of no consequence.

There was never a moment when a woman wondered what the men talked about when they gathered in the evening around the benches in the central piazza. Why should they care? It all came to nothing, generation after generation. The men would return home for dinner, then fall asleep before bedtime. Next day, coffee and morning chat.

Most likely they talked about the price of things, political and social scandals, football, weather. But what if they talked about scandalous things? Bank robberies? Sexual exploits?

It was just after the Allied bombings destroyed the city in 1943. Not a single building without damage. Some whole blocks turned to rubble. No food. Even the fig, orange, and lemon trees were gone.

## The Material Facts

I have a fair salary—good for the island, below poverty in some wealthy countries. I can buy as much food as I can eat. My rent and utilities are proportional to my income. I use public transportation, which is clean and efficient. I have no material wants.

"I am the child of people who lived through the Depression, a clean-your-plate kind of family."

As if we are not all those children.

## The Flamingo

Back to the lawnmower man. Back to that one flamingo, arrow-shaped, in flight above the estuary.

He's been riding this bus for twenty years. One stop. Every morning. Watching for something.

The bus driver says he's never hurt anyone. Says it like reassurance. Like we need to be told that someone's harmless just because they follow a pattern we don't understand, because they jabber prayers, because they're missing fingers.

Maybe the lawnmower man is the only one who's paying attention. Who sees the flamingos every morning and lets his heart skip a beat. Who boards a bus for one stop because that's the window where the miracle happens—the arrow-shaped bird, the pink gathered thousands, the water reflecting sky.

Maybe we're all riding the number 30 for one stop, watching for our flamingos, and the only difference is that some of us pretend we're going somewhere else.

## The Ordinary Is the Miracle

Hyper-realism as an antidote to the escape into science fiction and supernatural. The actual texture of lived experience. The man with no fingers. The old woman who woke up somewhere. Elena and her perfect vegetables. The bubble tea girl in her tropical plumage, surrounded by crows.

The sock in the courtyard. The "grazie" from across the way.

This is the material breaking through. This is the Real. Not the mystical vision in the palace of armor, not the voice from the crucifix, not the stigmata appearing on hands.

Just the number 30 bus. The canal. The flamingos.

The lawnmower man has been riding it for twenty years.
