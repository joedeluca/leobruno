## The Obscene Underside

The thing about ideology is it reveals itself not in what we talk about explicitly, but in what we do without thinking. The crude material reality. The body asserting itself despite our attempts to theorize it away.

Nobody discusses pubic hair grooming as ideology. That's precisely why it is.

The completely shaved look: a statement about hygiene, youth, accessibility, pornographic aesthetics that have become normalized. The fantasy here is that the body can be made completely manageable, completely controlled, returned to some pre-sexual state while paradoxically being hyper-sexualized.

The untrimmed natural: a statement about authenticity, rejection of corporate beauty standards, reclaiming the body from capitalism. The fantasy here is that there's some "natural" state outside of culture, that simply not doing something is a form of resistance.

The manicured middle ground: the trimmed, maintained, carefully considered arrangement. The fantasy of adult sophistication, of having thought about it without being controlled by it. Control disguised as casual maintenance.

## The Fantasy Creates What It Represses

Here's the Žižekian twist: each approach creates the very problem it claims to solve.

The complete removal that promises liberation from body hair anxiety creates _more_ anxiety—now you must maintain it, police the regrowth, achieve perfect smoothness. The absence becomes a presence that demands constant attention.

The natural untrimmed approach that rejects grooming standards becomes its own standard to maintain—you must resist the impulse to trim, must perform your naturalness, must prove you're not caving to pressure. Your non-action becomes an action that requires constant maintenance.

The "reasonable" middle ground that promises to avoid both extremes requires the most active management of all—deciding where to draw the line, maintaining that line, justifying your choices to yourself.

## Complicity

We're all complicit in whatever fantasy we choose. There's no escape position. No neutral ground. Even "I don't think about it" is a position that reveals something—usually privilege, usually male.

The person who says "I just do what feels good" hasn't escaped ideology—they've internalized it so completely they experience its dictates as natural preference. The compulsion arrives pre-packaged as desire.

## What This Has To Do With St. Francis

Francis stripped naked in the public square to renounce wealth. Big theatrical gesture. But he kept his boots for two more years. He managed his poverty the way we manage our pubic hair—carefully, ideologically, while pretending it's natural or holy or obvious.

The totally shaved Francis: complete renunciation, living in absolute poverty, refusing all comfort. Creating more anxiety about maintaining perfect poverty than wealth ever caused.

The natural Francis: just being poor, man, not making a big deal about it. Except he made it a huge deal. His whole identity depended on you knowing he was poor by choice.

The manicured Francis: choosing which poverty to perform and when. The cloak but not the boots. The grand gestures but not the systematic change.

## Vico and Practical Wisdom

Giambattista Vico argued we need practical wisdom derived from studying the totality of sciences and arts. What he meant was: understand the systems you're operating within before you think you can transcend them.

Francis didn't study the economics of poverty. He performed it. We don't study the cultural systems that make certain body modifications compulsory. We just comply or resist, usually while pretending we're freely choosing.

Vico would say: understand the forces shaping your choices before you call them choices.

## The Material Reality

Here's the crude material fact breaking through all the ideology: bodies grow hair. Hair grows back. Poverty exists. People suffer.

All our grooming—literal or metaphorical—doesn't change these facts. It just manages our relationship to them. The management becomes the point. We forget what we were managing.

Francis rebuilt a church building stone by stone. Gave his cloak to a knight. Kissed a leper. Never addressed the systemic causes of poverty, disease, or ecclesiastical corruption. Just managed his personal relationship to these things through visible acts.

We trim, shave, wax, or proudly don't—managing our personal relationship to beauty standards, gender norms, pornographic aesthetics. The structures producing these standards remain untouched.

## The Telephone Wire Is Charged With Distance

There's a reason I'm writing about this from Sardegna, communicating through screens. Physical intimacy makes lying harder. When you're disembodied, when there's distance, you can perform any version of yourself.

The telephone wire is charged not with connection but with distance. All I hear in the silence is: I'm going to leave you.

So we groom. We manage. We curate the version of ourselves we present. Online, on the page, in the public square. The ideology isn't in what we say we believe. It's in how we maintain the garden we claim to have let go wild. Or how we obsessively maintain the garden we claim is just practical. Or how we pretend there's no garden at all.

## What the Buddha Would Say

The Buddha would say: you're still attached. Just attached to different things. The French garden and the English garden both require gardeners. The shaved and the unshaven both require decisions. You think you've transcended the binary by choosing a side. You've just picked a different form of suffering.

True freedom isn't found in choosing perfectly trimmed, totally natural, or some middle ground. It's found in seeing through the compulsion to choose at all. In understanding that your "preference" isn't yours—it's a product of forces you haven't examined.

But who achieves that? Nobody I know. We're all grooming something, even if it's our image of not grooming.

## The Boots

Francis kept his boots. We all keep our boots. The question is whether we're honest about it.

The question is whether we can look at our gardens—French, English, wild, manicured—and see them as gardens. As constructions. As ideological statements we're making with meat and hair and choices we pretend are natural.

That's all I'm saying. How you attend to your pubic hair is an ideological statement. So is how you attend to poverty, holiness, your image, your life.

The fantasy helps create what it supposedly represses. We're all complicit. Nobody escapes.

The best we can do is see the garden for what it is.
