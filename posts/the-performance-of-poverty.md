## The Double Standard

Francis could refuse charity—that was holy. The poor should accept charity—that was humble. You see the problem? Francis's poverty was about personal spiritual attainment. It was a project. A performance. An identity he constructed and maintained through visible acts.

The Buddha would have seen this immediately: Francis wasn't transcending attachment, he was just inverting it. Attachment to poverty instead of wealth. Attachment to the identity of "poor holy man" instead of "rich merchant's son."

## What Francis Didn't Give

In one of the famous stories, Francis gives his cloak to a poor knight. Noble gesture, right? Except there was a beggar sitting right there—actually poor, not formerly-wealthy poor. Francis never saw him. Or saw him and chose the knight because the knight's humiliation was more interesting, more noble, more worthy of Francis's attention.

Francis kept his boots that day. Gave the cloak but kept the boots. Nobody writes about that part. He wore those boots for two more years before finally giving them away, and by then nobody was watching.

That's the thing about performed poverty—you get to choose which poverty to perform.

## The Empty Palace

Francis had a dream about a palace filled with armor. Everyone interprets this as a sign of his spiritual knighthood, his divine calling. But what if the palace was just empty? All weapons, no warriors. All structure, no substance.

Years later, when the Franciscan order grew huge and bureaucratic—thousands of monks, vast properties, political power—did Francis remember the empty palace? Did he understand that you can build an entire structure and have nobody inside it?

## Vico's Practical Wisdom

Giambattista Vico argued that education should develop "practical wisdom"—a well-rounded understanding that comes from studying the totality of sciences and arts. Francis had none of this. He was intuitive, emotional, theatrical. He had visions and heard voices.

This lack of practical wisdom is everywhere in American tech culture too. We have self-driving cars that don't work, computers we call telephones, "social" media that breeds isolation. Brilliant specialists with no philosophical grounding, no historical perspective, no understanding of human nature.

Francis was the medieval version: intensely focused on his spiritual project with no systematic understanding of poverty, suffering, or human psychology. The Buddha, by contrast, developed a detailed framework for understanding the mind. He tested theories. He was almost scientific in his approach.

## The Real Breaking Through

There's a Žižek concept I keep thinking about: how ideology reveals itself not in what we say explicitly, but in the obscene underside, the crude material reality we try to repress.

Francis's obscene underside was his privilege. He could perform poverty because he came from wealth. He could refuse medical care because he was young and strong. He could fast dramatically because he'd never experienced actual starvation. His entire theatrical poverty was built on the foundation of having options the truly poor never had.

The material reality breaking through: Francis died at 44, destroyed by his "holy" self-neglect. The poor die young too, but not by choice.

## What Would Actually Help

You want to help the poor? Study economics. Learn about systemic causes of poverty. Understand power structures. Develop practical solutions. Don't perform your spiritual journey on the backs of people who have no choice.

Francis rebuilt a literal church building when the metaphorical church was rotting from corruption. He spent years stacking stones when he could have been challenging the systems that created poverty in the first place.

But challenging systems is hard, unglamorous work. Stacking stones in a picturesque ruin while birds land on your shoulders? That's a fresco waiting to happen.

## The Boots

I keep coming back to those boots. Francis kept them for two years. That small, un-mentioned choice reveals more than all the theatrical stripping and bird-preaching combined.

We all keep our boots. We all perform our values while holding back something. The question is whether we're honest about it.

Francis wasn't. He built an entire spiritual identity around renunciation while carefully managing what he renounced and when and for whom. The actual poor watched this performance and got nothing from it except the theological assurance that their suffering was holy.

That's not compassion. That's not even shallow. As Nietzsche would say, it doesn't have the minimum qualities to be called an explanation at all.
