I was listening to Dirty Boulevard by Lou Reed and I noticed, as I always do, Lou's tender invocation of children and their being savaged with clothes hangers. And I thought: that's sweet. A guy like Lou Reed, all the money and the fame and black denim, taking the time to call out ordinary domestic atrocities. The tenderness of it. The specificity. Clothes hangers. Not fists. Clothes hangers.

I said to Elliott: Lou Reed must have been a real sweetie.

Elliott looked at his phone.

Elliott looked up from his phone.

_Uhhh._

And that's how the game started.

---

The rules, such as they are: you consider the work. You consider the person. You decide. The only disqualifying sin is one that is truly hurtful — not difficult, not annoying, not insufferable at press junkets. Truly hurtful. Everything else is just being human, which artists are, catastrophically, always.

---

Lou Reed. Not a sweetie. The work: transcendent. The man: by most accounts, a genuine piece of work — cruel to collaborators, brutal to Warhol's memory, slapped David Bowie (it was complicated), called Dylan a pretentious kike, and generally _complicated_ with everyone. Elliott's phone is merciless. And yet. Clothes hangers. You don't write that line without caring. The contradiction is the whole thing.

John Mellencamp. Not a sweetie either. Difficult. Famously. And yet: _Pink Houses_, _Jack and Diane_, the whole Midwest working-class project — this is a man who spent forty years being genuinely angry on behalf of people who weren't going to make it. The unhappiness isn't performance. It's the source. You can't write those songs happy.

Iggy Pop. Sweetie. This surprises everyone. Offstage: apparently warm, funny, genuinely kind. Onstage: human sacrifice. The separation is so complete it's almost philosophical.

Billy Bragg. Sweetie. Obvious. Next.

Bono. Nice person. Genuinely. Also: insufferable public persona. This is its own category — the sweetie whose sweetness has curdled into something that requires its own terminology. Not a not-sweetie. A sweetie who needs to be managed.

---

The game continues. It always continues. Elliott's phone is always ready. The question is always the same: does knowing change anything? And the answer is always: sometimes yes, sometimes no, and the inconsistency of your own standards is the point.

---

The thing is, Lou Reed wrote Dirty Boulevard because he gave a shit. The clothes hangers prove it. You can't fake the clothes hangers. Whatever he was in a room — and apparently he was a lot of things in rooms — on the page he was paying attention to the right things.

I still listen to Lou. But he was not a sweetie.

Elliott remains unconvinced.

The game continues.
