There really was never any such thing as "literary modernism". It was always modernisms. A plural.

Frank Kermode understood this. Paleo-modernism. Neo-modernism. Different projects. Different obsessions. Different gods.

Eliot and Williams hated each other and their work. This wasn't professional rivalry. This was fundamental disagreement about what poetry could be, should be, must be.

### Williams and The Red Wheelbarrow

Williams wrote <span class="poem-link" data-poem-id="the-red-wheelbarrow">The Red Wheelbarrow</span> as an assault on Eliot's poetics.

Against difficulty. Against allusiveness. Against historical obsessions. Against the defense of high culture as the only culture worth preserving.

The sentence structure is broken. Read it out loud—it's wretched. Deliberately so.

"So much depends on X" is the basic structure. Elaborated by a list of two things that modify X. That's it. That's the whole poem.

But read it as exclamation. Read it with the emphasis italicized: _So much depends..._

A more complex syntax emerges. An urgency. A stakes-raising that Eliot's elaborate constructions never quite achieve.

### The Polemics

Williams said: "Better to deprive birds of their song than to call them all nightingales."

Translation: Better to have no poetry at all than to pretend every chirp is a reference to Keats.

Do not say, Tom Eliot, that my poems are not poetry.

So much depends on the poem of the red wheelbarrow. It depends on itself. A defense of itself.

This is the argument: the poem doesn't need to reference anything outside itself. The wheelbarrow, the rain, the chickens—these aren't symbols. They're not standing in for something else. They're the thing itself.

The poem is what it says it is. No more. No less.

## Eliot's Response

<span class="poem-link" data-poem-id="mr-eliots-sunday-morning-service">Mr. Eliot's Sunday Morning Service</span> is everything Williams rejected.

Polyphiloprogenitive. Byzantine theology. Origen. Umbrian painters. Sweating presbyters. The garden-wall bees with their hairy bellies moving between staminate and pistilate.

And then: Sweeney. Shifting from ham to ham in his bath. The crude material body breaking through all the high culture.

The masters of the subtle schools are controversial, polymath. And also irrelevant to the man in the bath.

## What This Means

Two modernisms. Two visions of what poetry does.

Eliot: Poetry preserves fragments against ruins. It builds elaborate structures from the detritus of dead civilizations. It demands you know your Dante, your Sanskrit, your church fathers.

Williams: Poetry attends to what's here. The red wheelbarrow. The rain. The white chickens. These don't symbolize anything. They _are_ something. And that's enough.

Both are responses to the same crisis: the collapse of shared cultural reference points, the mechanization of life, the sense that meaning itself has fractured.

Eliot says: build careful structures from the fragments.

Williams says: attend to the fragment itself.

## Who Won?

Neither. Both. We got stuck with both visions and couldn't reconcile them.

The academy loved Eliot. All those references to explicate. All that difficulty to decode. Whole careers built on footnoting _The Waste Land_.

But Williams kept insisting: no ideas but in things. The thing itself. The wheelbarrow. The chickens.

And poets kept having to choose. High modernism or imagism. Difficulty or clarity. Europe or America. Dead languages or living speech.

A false binary. But one we're still trapped in.

Frank Kermode tried to map it. Paleo this, neo that. As if giving it names would resolve the tension.

It didn't. The battle continues. Every poem still has to decide: are we building elaborate structures, or are we attending to the red wheelbarrow?
