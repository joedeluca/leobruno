## All Weapons, No Warriors

In the version I keep thinking about, Francis's dream wasn't about promise—it was about absence. A palace built and furnished, every surface gleaming with polished steel, and not a single person inside. All weapons, no warriors. All structure, no substance.

He woke up terrified.

Years later, when the Franciscan order grew huge—thousands of monks, vast properties, political power, bureaucratic machinery—did he remember the empty palace? Did he understand that you can build an entire structure and have nobody inside it?

## What Brother Leo Said

According to Brother Leo's private notes (not the official hagiography), Francis told him the real dream years later, drunk on wine at dinner:

"I was trying to enter the palace but couldn't find the door. I circled it for hours. Then I woke up. That's all."

The armor story came later, when people needed it to make sense.

This rings true to me. We retrospectively impose meaning on our dreams, especially when we need them to justify our choices. The dream becomes the story that makes the subsequent life legible. The palace that couldn't be entered becomes the palace that promised knighthood.

## The Buddha and the Palace

The Buddha also had a palace dream, but his was backwards. He was born into an actual palace. His father built him three palaces, one for each season, trying to shield him from suffering. Entertainment, luxury, every desire fulfilled.

He left. Just walked out. No dream necessary. He saw through the palace to what it actually was: a beautiful prison built to prevent awakening.

Francis dreamed of a palace and spent his life trying to get in. The Buddha lived in three palaces and spent his life trying to get out.

## What Pietro Saw

Pietro Bernardone—Francis's father—also had a dream that night. In his dream, Francis was a servant in someone else's palace, sweeping floors.

When Francis told him about the armor and glory, Pietro said: "We dreamed different dreams about the same place."

They were both right.

## The Second Dream Nobody Mentions

Two weeks after the famous dream, Francis had another. The palace was on fire. All the armor melting. He stood outside watching it burn, and he was relieved.

This dream he never told anyone. But it was the one that decided him.

Why don't we talk about this one? Because it contradicts the narrative. Can't have the hero relieved that his sacred calling is burning. Can't have ambivalence, regret, second-guessing. Saints don't doubt. They don't dream of destruction and feel relief.

But people do. People dream of the structures they're building going up in flames and wake up grateful.

## Vico and the Fantastic Image

Giambattista Vico argued that "primitive times produce primitive knowledge that is a projection of the barbaric mind—fantastic images of gods based on fear, guilt, and terror."

Francis's time wasn't primitive in Vico's sense, but the mechanism is the same. The dream of the palace was a projection. A fantastic image based on Francis's fear, guilt, and terror about his life direction.

Merchant's son. Supposed to take over the family business. Capture, imprisonment, illness. Existential crisis. And then: a dream that makes it all make sense. A dream that transforms fear into calling, guilt into mission, terror into spiritual warfare.

The fantastic image creates its own truth. Francis dreamed of a palace, interpreted it as divine instruction, built an order, and thousands of people filled that order. The palace became real because he believed it was real.

But was anyone actually inside it? Or just wearing the armor, performing the role, circling the structure looking for the door?

## The Empty Monastery

I visited a Franciscan monastery in Assisi. Beautiful building. Ancient stones. Incredible frescoes. Chapel with perfect acoustics. Gift shop. Museum.

Three monks. Three. Rattling around in a structure built for hundreds.

The palace was empty.

Not because of vocations crisis or secularization or modernity. Because Francis built a structure based on a dream he may have made up or at least heavily edited, and that structure was always, fundamentally, empty. All weapons, no warriors. All roles, no people.

## What the Architect Said

In one version of the dream, Francis meets the architect of the palace.

Francis: "Who is this for?"
Architect: "You."
Francis: "But I don't want a palace."
Architect: "I know. That's why you're the only one who can have it."

Francis woke up angry. Spent months trying to understand. Never did.

This is the most honest version. The dream that doesn't resolve. The symbol that doesn't symbolize anything useful. Just anger and confusion and a sense of being trapped by something you didn't ask for.

## Tommy Lee's Palace

Tommy Lee—drummer for Mötley Crüe—also had a palace. Literal mansion. Pool full of Ferraris (he drove them in there). Every excess imaginable. The palace of hedonism, built and furnished with every weapon of desire.

But Tommy Lee wasn't trying to get into his palace or justify it with dreams. He was already in it. Living it. Using every weapon. And then, having used them all, he walked out. Cleaned up. Kept drumming.

He didn't need the palace to be spiritually significant. Didn't need it to mean something. It was just a palace. He lived in it until he didn't.

Francis couldn't do that. The palace had to mean something. Had to be a sign, a calling, a divine instruction. So he spent his life interpreting the dream, building the structure, filling it with armor no one could wear.

## The Question

What's the difference between a palace that houses the real thing and a palace that houses the performance of the thing?

Francis's order: thousands of monks performing poverty. Carefully managed poverty. Poverty as identity, as statement, as spiritual credential. The armor of poverty. All weapons, no warriors.

The Buddha's order: monks actually attempting to extinguish craving. Not performing its extinction, but working toward it. Some succeed, most don't, but at least they're honest about what they're attempting.

Tommy Lee's mansion: no pretense. This is excess. This is pleasure. This is everything. Then it's not enough. Then it's different. But never pretending to be something else.

## The Door That Doesn't Exist

Francis circled the palace for hours, looking for the door. This detail—from Leo's notes, not the official version—is everything.

He was outside the thing he was supposed to enter. He knew it was for him. But he couldn't get in. He couldn't find the door because the door didn't exist. The palace was sealed. A beautiful, gleaming structure with no entrance. No exit. No interior.

Just exterior. Just appearance. Just the dream of what a palace should be.

And Francis spent his life standing outside it, describing it to others, telling them about the armor inside, the glory, the spiritual knighthood. Never mentioning that he never found the door. Never got in. Never saw the interior.

Built a whole order of people standing outside the same palace, circling it, looking for the entrance, performing the conviction that they'd found it.

## What We Build

We all build structures based on dreams we half-remember and heavily edit. We interpret those dreams to justify what we were going to do anyway. We fill those structures with people performing roles nobody can actually live.

And then we stand outside them, circling, looking for the door, convinced that our inability to enter is a personal failing rather than a feature of the structure itself.

The palace is always empty. The armor is always unwearable. The door is never where we think it is.

The question is whether we're honest about it.

Francis wasn't. He made it into legend, into calling, into eight hundred years of people repeating the dream as if it meant what he said it meant. As if the palace were real. As if anyone actually got inside.

The Buddha would say: stop circling the palace. Stop looking for the door. The palace is a construct. The dream is a projection. There's nothing to enter because there's nothing there.

Tommy Lee would say: I had a palace. It was cool. Then it wasn't. I left. Not a metaphor. Just a house.

## The Flamingo Over the Estuary

This morning on the number 30 bus, I saw a flamingo in flight over the estuary. Arrow-shaped. Pink. Singular against the grey sky. My heart skipped a beat.

That's the only palace that matters. The one that appears for a moment and disappears. The one you don't build. The one you don't fill with armor. The one you don't circle looking for doors.

The one that flies and is gone and was never yours to begin with.

Francis dreamed of a palace filled with armor. I dream of watching flamingos from a bus and having that be enough.

No structure. No interpretation. No spiritual significance. Just the arrow-shaped bird and the skipped heartbeat and the next stop approaching.

The palace was always empty. The only question is how long it takes us to stop circling it, stop looking for the door, and watch the flamingos instead.
